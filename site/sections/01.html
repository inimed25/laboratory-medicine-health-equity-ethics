Where does laboratory care fit?

Health and health care disparities are documented across populations and settings. Laboratory medicine is part of the care pathway in which these disparities can arise: who is offered testing, whether collection and return are feasible, and how results are interpreted and followed up.

The FIT dashboard gives us a starting point for understanding a difference in completion. Its importance does not depend on already knowing its full explanation. The central distinction is between identifying a pattern and understanding the processes that contribute to it.

Match each statement to its main emphasis

These concepts overlap. Social conditions can contribute to differences in care and health outcomes, and some disparities reflect inequities. Definitions also vary across sources; the matches identify each statement's main emphasis.

In the FIT case: Completion is a health care process. The dashboard does not directly measure cancer incidence or mortality. The gap deserves attention while its contributing processes remain uncertain.

Health care equity describes a fair opportunity to access, receive, and benefit from appropriate, high-quality care without unfair barriers.

Beat 3 of 5

What happened before and after the specimen?

A valid result depends on more than an assay working correctly. For FIT, a patient needs an opportunity to access screening, receive an offer or order, obtain a kit, collect and return a specimen, and receive appropriate interpretation and follow-up.

Social determinants of health are the conditions in which people live, learn, work, and age, together with broader forces shaping those conditions. Structural determinants include institutional, economic, and policy arrangements shaping those opportunities.

In this fictional case, clinic hours, collection instructions, return options, or missing outside-system testing are possible explanations. None has been demonstrated by the opening dashboard.

What else can shape the diagnostic journey?

Relevant conditions may include insurance coverage and cost, preferred language and available language services, disability and accessibility, housing, working conditions and paid leave, caregiving, digital access, and availability of clinical services. Institutional policies and broader financing or eligibility rules can shape these conditions. Incarceration and immigration-related policies may also affect access and continuity of care.

These are possible influences, not assumptions about an individual or explanations established by a demographic category. Their relevance depends on the patient, setting, and diagnostic pathway.

Optional reflection: Where could a patient leave this pathway without ever appearing among the specimens received by the laboratory?

Discussion: Before an order, before obtaining a kit, or before specimen return. A dataset of received specimens cannot describe everyone who might have benefited from testing.

Beat 4 of 5

What does the category leave out?

The 64% completion rate describes a group, not every person in it. An area-level deprivation measure does not establish an individual patient's income, circumstances, understanding, or behavior. People within the category may have very different experiences.

What can the pathologist contribute?

The laboratory can perform FIT accurately while some people never obtain or return a kit, or never receive follow-up after a result. Laboratory teams contribute knowledge about how testing is designed, performed, and reported. Their authority and opportunities to contribute vary by setting.

Relationship to the issueExamples
Direct responsibility within the laboratorySpecimen acceptance criteria, assay performance, and reporting design
Influence through shared workflowsCollection instructions, specimen-return arrangements, and ordering pathways
Contribution through partnershipDiscussions with clinical teams, patients, informatics colleagues, and community partners about access and follow-up

These roles can overlap. Collection instructions, for example, may be managed by the laboratory in one setting and jointly with clinical services in another.

Evidence across settings

Published findings can identify questions worth asking locally, but patient populations, resources, and workflows may differ between settings. External evidence can inform understanding without establishing that the same mechanism explains a local gap.

Things to keep in mind

An observed disparity does not establish its cause. Identifying a pattern matters even while its contributing processes remain uncertain.

A group average does not describe every individual. Circumstances vary within demographic and geographic categories.

An accurate result is one part of diagnostic care. Access before testing and interpretation and follow-up afterward also shape its value.

Equity glossary

Health disparity: A population difference in health status or outcomes, interpreted using the source's definition; some definitions emphasize disadvantage and some include differences in care.

Health care disparity: A population difference in access to, use of, or quality of care, interpreted using the source's definition and relevant context.

Health inequity: A difference understood as unfair or unjust; definitions often also emphasize avoidability or remediability.

Health equity: Fair opportunity to attain health and receive appropriate care without unfair barriers.

Social determinants of health: Conditions and broader forces shaping how people live and their opportunities for health.

Structural determinants: Broader institutional, economic, political, and social arrangements shaping social position and opportunities.
