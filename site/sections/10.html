The pathologist's role in equitable diagnosis

A test can be analytically accurate while its benefits and burdens are distributed unequally. Who receives testing, what an assay detects, which reference interval is applied, and when information becomes available all shape its usefulness in care.

Pathologists contribute by explaining both the information a test provides and its limits, in conversation with clinical teams, laboratory staff, patients, and informatics colleagues. That expertise can help others recognize when a testing or reporting choice may affect patients differently.

Understanding a test includes understanding its limits—and whom those limits may affect.

Apply this to a testing pathway you know

Optional, unscored exercise: choose one familiar test and sketch the pathway from considering testing to acting on the result. You can think through the prompts or make your own notes; this page does not collect them.

  1. Who is missing? Who might never appear in the laboratory dashboard, and what is its starting denominator?
  2. Where might burdens differ? Consider collection, cost, language, transport, privacy, and the ability to receive or act on results.
  3. What would you investigate? Name one additional data source and one perspective that would help distinguish possible explanations.
  4. Who should help? Identify relevant laboratory staff, clinical or operational partners, and patients or community members. Whose input would influence the decision?
  5. What would you monitor? Choose a process measure, a stratified outcome, and a balancing measure for one small proposed change.
One possible approach: a specimen-return pathway

Begin with all kit requests, then distinguish dispatch, confirmed delivery where available, specimen receipt, valid result, and follow-up. Combine records with feedback from people whose kits were not returned. A small change might be offering a locally feasible return option selected with patients and staff. Track use of that option, completion across relevant groups, and added travel or staff workload. This is an investigation plan, not evidence that the change will work everywhere.

You do not need to prove the full cause of a disparity before asking a useful question. State what you know, what remains uncertain, and how the next step could improve understanding.

Optional further reading

These selected resources offer starting points for deeper inquiry. They are optional, not additional assessed content. Sources include empirical studies, professional guidance, and broader conceptual work; they serve different purposes. Case-specific references remain alongside each case.

Foundations and the diagnostic journey

World Health Organization — Social determinants of health. An accessible overview connecting living conditions and broader social arrangements with health inequities. Useful for placing laboratory access within a wider context.

National Academies — Improving Diagnosis in Health Care (2015). A broader diagnostic-safety report on the diagnostic process, teamwork, and patients' participation. Relevant to the pathologist's collaborative role, although not a laboratory-equity guideline.

Access, selection, and timing

Disparities in Biomarker Testing and Clinical Trial Enrollment Among Patients With Lung, Breast, or Colorectal Cancers in the United States (2022). An empirical example of unequal receipt of clinically relevant molecular testing, including testing before first-line treatment. Supports discussion of access and timing without establishing every mechanism behind missing testing.

Schoneich et al. — Incidence of Newborn Drug Testing and Variations by Birthing Parent Race and Ethnicity Before and After Recreational Cannabis Legalization (2023). A contrasting example: equity concerns can involve unequal exposure to testing and its consequences, rather than only insufficient testing.

Assays, reference populations, and reporting

AMP/CAP — Recommendations for Clinical CYP2C9 Genotyping Allele Selection (2019). A laboratory-specific example of how target selection and population allele frequencies matter beyond accuracy for included targets. These are gene-specific recommendations, not a universal panel-design rule.

ADLM — Guidance document on incorporating gender diversity in pathology and laboratory medicine (2026). Covers analyte-specific interpretation, reference intervals, and EHR/LIS issues. Its hormone-related interval recommendations have defined scope and do not supply one demographic rule for every analyte.

Vyas, Eisenstein, and Jones — Hidden in Plain Sight: Reconsidering the Use of Race Correction in Clinical Algorithms (2020). A perspective broadening the discussion of how race enters clinical decision rules. Useful historical framing; individual algorithms and recommendations require current, topic-specific evidence.

AI data and quality improvement

Chang et al. — Racial differences in laboratory testing as a potential mechanism for bias in AI (2024). Links documented testing differences to potential problems with laboratory-dependent AI labels. The study measured testing patterns, rather than harm from a deployed model.

CDC — Updated Operational Guidance for Implementing Recommendations on Testing for Hepatitis C Virus Infection (2023). A practical laboratory-workflow resource on incomplete testing, single-visit collection, and automatic RNA testing. Supports the QI workflow discussion; subgroup equity effects remain a separate question.
