Case 1: What does the AI training label represent?

A team develops a model to identify a condition using EHR and laboratory data. Patients with a qualifying abnormal test result are labeled positive. All other patients, including those never tested, are labeled negative.

Question 1

Before using these labels to train a model of underlying disease, which next step best addresses the problem?

Choose an answer

Best answer: D. Untested patients are not established negatives. Reviewing label validity and who gets tested addresses the problem before model fitting. Reweighting and subgroup evaluation may be useful later, but cannot by themselves repair an invalid reference label. Restricting to tested patients changes the population and does not establish performance for those never tested. The final modeling approach depends on the intended use and available ascertainment data.

Evidence and limitations
  • Racial differences in laboratory testing as a potential mechanism for bias in AI: A matched cohort analysis in emergency department visits

The study supports reviewing testing-dependent labels; the proposed development steps are case-based reasoning.

A useful interdisciplinary discussion connects the data team's label definitions, clinicians' ordering context, and the laboratory's knowledge of canceled, rejected, or outside-system testing.

Evidence and scope

Real-world basis: Chang and colleagues (2024) studied Black and White adult emergency-department visits at two U.S. teaching hospitals. After matching on age, recorded sex, chief complaint, and triage score, Black patients had lower rates of CBC, metabolic-panel, and blood-culture testing; troponin testing showed the opposite direction. The authors discussed how testing-dependent labels could introduce AI bias. They did not evaluate harm from a deployed model or establish why each ordering difference occurred. The model and label definition in this case are hypothetical.

Reference: Racial differences in laboratory testing as a potential mechanism for bias in AI: A matched cohort analysis in emergency department visits.

Case 2: The result did not change, but the flag did

A patient's hemoglobin result is unchanged. After a legal-sex field is updated, the result changes from flagged high to unflagged. The patient is receiving testosterone. The laboratory and EHR maintain separate fields for gender identity, sex assigned at birth, and legal sex.

What changed?

In this hypothetical case, the LIS selected a different reference interval using the updated legal-sex field. The unchanged value falls between the two upper limits. A flag reflects comparison with a configured interval; its change does not resolve clinical interpretation.

Question 2

What should the laboratory clarify before judging whether the changed hemoglobin flag is appropriate?

Choose an answer

Best answer: C. First establish what the software did and whether the applied interval fits the analyte and relevant physiology. Hormone exposure and duration can matter for hemoglobin interpretation. Administrative-field accuracy or symptom review alone does not establish that the comparison interval is suitable. This is a reporting review, not a patient-specific diagnosis.

Evidence and limitations
  • Impact of sex used for assignment of reference intervals in a population of patients taking gender-affirming hormones
  • Providing Inclusive Care for Transgender Patients: Capturing Sex and Gender in Electronic Medical Record
  • ADLM — Gender Diversity guidance

These sources support analyte-specific interpretation and review of EHR/LIS interval logic. The software event is fictional; the case does not establish patient harm.

Evidence and scope

Real-world basis: A 2024 study examined how sex used for reference-interval assignment affects abnormal-result flags in patients taking gender-affirming hormones. ADLM’s 2026 guidance also explicitly discusses legal-sex fields, EHR/LIS logic, and hemoglobin reporting. Its recommendations for selected analytes address patients stably receiving hormone therapy for at least six months; interpretation varies by analyte and clinical context. A changed flag may be appropriate—for example, a male hemoglobin interval in someone stably receiving testosterone. The exact software event here is hypothetical, and the case does not establish patient harm or a measured population disparity. Its equity concern is whether reporting systems support appropriate interpretation for gender-diverse patients. ADLM guidance is not a CAP accreditation requirement.

Things to keep in mind

Improvement is not automatically improved equity. Overall metrics can improve while existing disparities persist.

Not tested is not the same as negative. Absence of a measurement does not establish absence of a condition.

A flag is a software comparison. A measurement can be accurate while its flag is misleading because of an unsuitable reference interval.

A demographic difference is a finding, not its explanation. Multiple clinical, social, and structural processes can contribute.

One perspective cannot represent an entire population. Experiences vary within and between populations.

Takeaway: Data and reporting choices shape what becomes visible.

Case 3: Covered testing, unequal privacy

This patient and local workflow are fictional.

During an interdisciplinary review, a pathology trainee hears about a 22-year-old patient considering indicated chlamydia and gonorrhea testing while covered by a parent’s insurance plan. The patient is concerned that a billing communication might reveal the visit or testing to the policyholder. The clinic can collect the specimen and the laboratory can perform the assay, but the patient asks to postpone testing until the communication pathway is clarified.

The laboratory’s dashboard begins with specimens received and shows no processing problem. The review includes laboratory, clinical, billing, and privacy staff because each sees a different part of the pathway.

Question 3

What does this case add to the interpretation of testing access?

Choose an answer

Best answer: B. Usable access includes the ability to seek testing with acceptable privacy. Laboratory-result confidentiality and billing communications involve different systems. This patient’s concern warrants clarification with the relevant teams; it does not establish a population-level cause.

Evidence and limitations
  • Leichliter et al. — Confidentiality Issues and Use of STD Services (CDC, 2017)
  • HHS — Uses and Disclosures for Treatment, Payment, and Health Care Operations

The CDC study supports the confidentiality-and-access concern; HHS guidance provides communication and payment context. Neither documents this fictional incident.

Evidence and scope

A 2017 CDC analysis of the 2013–2015 National Survey of Family Growth found that confidentiality concerns were associated with lower self-reported chlamydia testing among female respondents. Its confidentiality question covered sexually experienced respondents aged 15–17 and those aged 18–25 on a parent’s private insurance plan. The cross-sectional survey did not establish causation or isolate billing communications as the mechanism.

HHS guidance describes permitted treatment and payment disclosures and confidential-communication requests. Options depend on the applicable rules and setting; this case does not establish a legal violation or recommend a blanket delay of result release.

  • Leichliter et al. — Confidentiality Issues and Use of STD Services (CDC, 2017)
  • HHS — Uses and Disclosures for Treatment, Payment, and Health Care Operations

Sources checked October 4, 2026.

Reflection: What becomes invisible when a laboratory measures only patients whose specimens arrive? How does concern about disclosure differ from inability to pay?

Coverage is not automatically usable access. Financial protection and privacy can affect the same testing decision in different ways.

Privacy is part of the diagnostic pathway. Result reporting and billing communications involve different systems and responsibilities.
