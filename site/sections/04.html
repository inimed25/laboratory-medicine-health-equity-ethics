Section 4 · One case

Bias before the specimen

Before a specimen reaches the laboratory, ordering rules and assumptions can shape who is considered for testing. Hemoglobinopathy testing is one example of this broader issue.

Hypothetical case

A pathology resident reviews a prenatal hemoglobinopathy-testing order set. Its decision support uses the patient's recorded race or ethnicity to determine whether to display a testing recommendation.

A colleague explains: “Some hemoglobinopathies are more common in particular ancestry groups, so this helps identify patients who need testing.”

Question 1

What is the central equity concern with this approach?

Choose an answer

Best answer: D. Population patterns can be informative, but a demographic category is an imperfect proxy for an individual's genetics. When that category determines who is offered testing, its limitations can affect access before a specimen is collected.

Why the other answers are less appropriate
  • A. Population frequencies can inform laboratory practice. The problem is using recorded race or ethnicity as a gatekeeper for an individual testing offer.
  • B. Allowing multiple categories may improve demographic recording, but still does not establish an individual’s genetic ancestry or carrier status.
  • C. Consistent analysis applies after collection. It does not correct unequal access created by the rule deciding who is offered testing.
Evidence and limitations
  • ACOG — Hemoglobinopathies in Pregnancy

See the case evidence note for the population, scope, and limitations of this source. Case interpretation does not establish an individual cause.

Takeaway: A demographic shortcut can become a testing barrier. The issue extends beyond how a laboratory analyzes specimens to how patients become eligible for testing.

Real-world basis and screening scope

Real-world basis

ACOG's 2022 advisory explicitly moved away from a race/ethnicity-based approach. It recommends offering hemoglobinopathy testing to people planning pregnancy or at the initial prenatal visit when prior results are unavailable for interpretation. The advisory identifies race and self-reported ethnicity as poor proxies for genetics. The order set above is hypothetical; the recommendation concerns this reproductive screening context and is not a claim that every laboratory test should be offered universally.

Reference: ACOG — Hemoglobinopathies in Pregnancy.
