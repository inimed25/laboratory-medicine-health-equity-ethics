Question 1

What is the most important limitation of judging this program using laboratory acceptance and turnaround time alone?

Choose an answer

Best answer: B. Acceptance and turnaround describe specimens that arrived. Beginning the dashboard there leaves earlier losses unexamined. Track the full pathway to locate missed transitions, then investigate why they occur.

Why the other answers are less appropriate
  • A. A gap between dispatch and receipt identifies a transition to investigate. It does not establish that home collection itself is unsuitable.
  • C. Acceptance among returned specimens gives no evidence about whether people who did not return kits still needed testing.
  • D. Changing the denominator can describe overall completion more clearly, but it does not identify whether delivery, collection, or return barriers caused losses.

Which next investigation would best distinguish kit-delivery problems from collection or return barriers?

Choose an answer

Best answer: A. The largest measured gap emerges between dispatch and specimen receipt. Linking delivery information with collection and return experiences helps separate the steps within that gap. Returned-specimen records and successful users can inform the review, but leave out people who never reached those stages. Contact methods should be feasible and protect privacy; an investigation may still leave some causes unknown.

Why the other answers are less appropriate
  • B. Rejection records describe specimens that arrived. They cannot directly explain kits or specimens that never reached the laboratory.
  • C. Transit times among returned specimens inform the transport step, but exclude unsuccessful delivery and patients who never collected or returned a specimen.
  • D. Successful users provide useful feedback, but relying on them alone risks missing the experiences of people who could not complete the pathway.

Shared debrief

Laboratory records describe an important part of the pathway, but not everyone who sought testing. Linking program and laboratory records makes earlier losses visible; stratifying those transitions can reveal differences in completion.

The pathologist contributes knowledge of specimen requirements, acceptance, and laboratory records. Program staff and patients contribute knowledge of delivery, collection, and return experiences. The case's address categories describe circumstances relevant to the workflow, rather than a patient's motivation or reliability.

Takeaway: Who reaches the laboratory shapes what laboratory data can show. Good performance among received specimens can coexist with unequal completion earlier in the pathway.

Real-world evidence and case boundaries

Real-world basis

Workflow feasibility: FDA's 2023 authorization supports home-collected specimens shipped to a laboratory. It does not establish equitable completion.

Documented completion differences: Manavi and Hodson's UK observational study reported return of 3,099 of 5,310 requested kits (58.4%). Kits requested from less deprived neighborhoods were more likely to be returned in adjusted analysis. The design did not establish why patients failed to return kits.

Patient experience: A 2024 qualitative study of 100 service users found that convenience and privacy could facilitate postal testing. Some participants' living situations made home delivery less private, so collecting a kit elsewhere was preferable. Limited representation of digitally excluded people constrains its applicability.

Benefits and scope: A 2017 London randomized trial increased STI testing uptake with an online postal service (50.0% versus 26.6%). Eligibility required internet access and ability to read English. It does not establish access for excluded populations.

Case boundary: Our counts, address categories, and subgroup gap remain hypothetical. These studies do not directly demonstrate lower return rates among people with temporary or changing addresses. Neighborhood deprivation and housing instability are different measures.

Step 3 of 3 · Following the result beyond the laboratory

A reported result is not the end of the pathway

This local workflow and the patients are fictional.

A pathology trainee joins a review of STI testing across a community clinic and a youth correctional facility. Both services use the same laboratory, which meets its reporting target. The community clinic’s usual follow-up pathway asks patients to check their portal or call to arrange care.

In this fictional review, some patients in custody cannot independently use that pathway, and some recently released patients do not have reliable private phone access or a stable address. Other patients can readily receive messages and arrange follow-up. These circumstances vary within each setting; incarceration or housing status alone does not establish an individual’s access.

Question 3

What makes this an equity concern beyond the laboratory’s reporting performance?

Choose an answer

Best answer: C. A patient-dependent follow-up pathway can create different opportunities to benefit from the same reported result. Examine communication access and coordination across release or transfer. Unknown follow-up must remain distinct from documented absence of treatment.

Why the other answers are less appropriate
  • A. Meeting a shared reporting target does not establish that patients have comparable access to messages or the ability to arrange follow-up.
  • B. Unavailable follow-up documentation means treatment status is unknown. It does not establish that a released patient went untreated.
  • D. Turnaround-time comparisons describe timing. They cannot alone establish communication barriers, their causal role, or a treatment disparity.
Evidence and limitations

Wolf et al. — Screening for Chlamydia and Gonorrhea in Youth Correctional Facilities, Utah, USA (2024)

The study documents a transition before results returned and unknown treatment after release. It does not establish a follow-up disparity compared with community patients.

Real-world basis and limits

Wolf and colleagues studied screening in 12 Utah youth correctional facilities during October 2021–May 2023. Among youth testing positive for chlamydia, gonorrhea, or both, 25.35% were released before results were known. Those treated in custody received treatment within two days of results. Released youth were referred to local health departments; treatment data after release were unavailable.

This supports examining the transition between a reported result and care. It does not show that released youth went untreated, compare follow-up with community patients, or establish phone or housing barriers as the cause. The communication constraints in this teaching case are fictional.
