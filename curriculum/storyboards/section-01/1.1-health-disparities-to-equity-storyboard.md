# 1.1 Storyboard — From Health Disparities to Health Equity

> **Status:** Approved direction; pre-build storyboard
>
> **Target time:** 10–15 minutes
>
> **Design rule:** Every interaction must make the learner predict, distinguish, investigate, interpret, or decide. No decorative interactivity.
>
> **Evidence:** See `curriculum/evidence-maps/section-01/1.1-health-disparities-to-equity.md`.

## Experience arc

**Notice → Name → Zoom out → Choose a lens → PERSON → POPULATION → PLACE → MECHANISM → ACTION → Laboratory transfer**

The learner starts with an unexplained difference in diagnostic data and returns to the same case at the end with a more disciplined approach.

## 1 — Cold open: You found a difference

**Learner sees:** A fictional laboratory dashboard showing two populations with different rates of receiving/completing a diagnostic test.

**Learner does:** Answers: *What does this establish?* Options include inequity, biology, inappropriate care, or “a difference has been observed; more information is needed.”

**Reveal:** **OBSERVATION ≠ EXPLANATION.** A signal has been identified, not its mechanism.

**Purpose:** Expose premature causal interpretation before teaching terminology.

**Visual:** Realistic, minimal laboratory dashboard. Values must also be available as text; color cannot carry meaning alone.

## 2 — Words change the claim

**Learner sees:** Short statements about the opening case rather than static definition cards.

**Learner does:** Matches statements to **difference/inequality, disparity, healthcare disparity, inequity, equity**.

**Reveal:** Definitions after the attempt, plus: **Terminology varies.** Sources do not all use “disparity” identically; determine how the source defines the term.

**Purpose:** Show that vocabulary changes the strength/type of claim being made.

**Evidence:** Braveman; WHO; Healthy People; NIMHD terminology; Ma, Agrawal & Salhi.

## 3 — Zoom out: Where could this difference come from?

Start at the specimen/result and progressively reveal:

**Laboratory → Clinical encounter → Healthcare access → Conditions of daily life → Structural context**

**Learner does:** Places possible influences such as assay availability, transportation, insurance design, work schedule, specimen transport, language access, neighborhood conditions, and institutional policy.

**Reveal:** Laboratory datasets can reflect processes operating well beyond the analytical phase.

**Purpose:** Introduce SDOH and structural determinants because they are needed to reason through the case.

**Evidence:** Healthy People 2030; WHO SDOH framework.

## 4 — Framework selector

Teach frameworks through the questions they help answer:

- **What domains of social conditions should I consider?** → Healthy People 2030
- **Through what pathways can structural conditions produce differences?** → WHO structural-determinants framework
- **At what levels could intervention occur?** → ecological/nested-level thinking
- **Where can these forces enter diagnostic care?** → Diagnostic Journey (**DIRECTOR LENS**)

**Learner does:** Matches question to useful framework.

**Reveal:** Frameworks are tools emphasizing different questions, not competing “correct answers.”

**Visual:** Original simplified schematics; do not reproduce copyrighted published figures.

# Five-part reasoning sequence

A persistent visual gradually assembles:

**PERSON → POPULATION → PLACE → MECHANISM → ACTION**

## 5 — PERSON: Population risk ≠ individual destiny

**Scenario:** A study finds higher ACE scores are associated with higher average rates of later poor health outcomes. The learner meets one patient with a high ACE score.

**Learner does:** Chooses what the population finding permits them to conclude about this individual.

**Reveal:** Two overlapping distributions. **A population signal can be real while individual outcomes remain uncertain.**

Rapid follow-up: *Should population evidence therefore be ignored?* → No. It can inform questions, risk assessment, prevention, and investigation without replacing information about the person.

**Purpose:** Separate population association from individual prediction.

**Evidence:** Baldwin et al. 2021; Austin et al. 2024; ecological-inference literature.

**Time:** 60–90 seconds. ACEs remain an example, not a sublesson.

## 6 — POPULATION: Open the category

**Learner sees:** One aggregate fictional population category.

**Learner does:** Selects **Look inside the group**. The aggregate expands to show heterogeneity in potentially relevant dimensions such as geography, socioeconomic conditions, ancestry, immigration history, access, language, or exposures.

Then: *Was the aggregate category useless?*

**Reveal:** **A population category can reveal a pattern without fully describing the people within it.** Aggregation can be informative and can also conceal meaningful heterogeneity.

**Evidence:** Kader & Chebli; Eisenhower et al.; measurement literature.

**Visual:** One aggregate distribution decomposes into variation; avoid stereotyped avatars.

## 7 — PLACE: Does the published disparity exist here?

**Learner sees:** A fictional published disparity followed by two settings differing in population, referral structure, geography, test availability, insurance environment, specimen logistics, and existing interventions.

**Learner does:** Decides what a laboratory director should conclude locally.

**Reveal:** **External evidence → Local data → Local context/mechanism → Decision**

External evidence makes a question worth investigating; it does not establish that the same disparity, magnitude, or mechanism exists locally.

**DIRECTOR LENS:** **Investigate, don't presume.**

**Nuance:** Local data can themselves contain missingness, selection, categorization, and measurement problems.

**Evidence:** Brownson et al.; transportability/external-validity literature; Ponce et al.

## 8 — MECHANISM: Same pattern, different causes

Return to the opening difference. Reveal several pathways capable of producing it:

**Need/exposure → care seeking → ordering → specimen collection → transport → assay availability → reporting → follow-up**

**Learner does:** Attempts to identify the cause from limited data.

**Reveal:** **You don't know yet.** Multiple mechanisms fit the observation.

The learner then chooses which additional information would distinguish among mechanisms.

**Purpose:** Teach observation → hypotheses → investigation rather than observation → attribution.

## 9 — ACTION: Now fix it

**Learner sees:** Tempting interventions such as order-set changes, expanded testing, screening, navigation, logistics changes, education, or targeted data collection.

**Learner does:** Tries to choose an intervention before mechanism/context are sufficiently known.

**Reveal:** **MEASUREMENT ≠ INTERVENTION.**

Additional mechanism/context information is then supplied and the learner chooses again.

**Purpose:** Make the learner experience why the same intervention may be appropriate or misguided depending on mechanism.

**Evidence:** Garg et al.; Brownson et al.; ACE-screening literature.

## 10 — Laboratory transfer: The specimen has a history

A specimen/result enters the laboratory and the interface rewinds:

**Result ← Assay ← Specimen ← Collection ← Ordering ← Encounter ← Access ← Exposure/social context**

Then plays forward:

**Result → Interpretation → Communication → Treatment/follow-up → Outcome → Data/surveillance**

**Learner does:** Identifies stages where a laboratory director may have **CONTROL**, **INFLUENCE**, or need **PARTNERSHIP/ADVOCACY**. Explain that boundaries vary by institution.

**Reveal:** Laboratory data are generated within a diagnostic system. Understanding an apparent inequity may require examining who had the opportunity to enter that system, what occurred within it, and what happened after the result.

**Purpose:** Transfer the public-health reasoning directly into laboratory leadership.

## 11 — Director challenge: Return to the opening case

Show the exact opening dashboard again.

The learner completes:

### WHAT DO YOU KNOW?
An observed difference exists in these data.

### WHAT DON'T YOU KNOW?
Potential mechanism, causality, representativeness, whether the difference constitutes inequity, relevant within-group heterogeneity, and transportability beyond the dataset/setting.

### WHAT WOULD YOU INVESTIGATE NEXT?
Choose data that discriminate among plausible mechanisms along the diagnostic pathway.

Then assemble:

- **PERSON** — Population risk is not individual destiny.
- **POPULATION** — Group averages can conceal heterogeneity.
- **PLACE** — Evidence may not transport unchanged across settings.
- **MECHANISM** — Difference does not establish cause.
- **ACTION** — Measurement does not automatically identify intervention.

### Final director question

> **What does the evidence suggest we should investigate in our own diagnostic system?**

# End matter

## Five takeaways

1. Observe differences without prematurely assigning cause.
2. Use population evidence without converting probabilities into assumptions about individuals.
3. Treat demographic categories as potentially informative but incomplete.
4. Use external evidence to generate locally testable questions.
5. Understand mechanism and context before choosing an intervention.

## Assessment

Use **5–6 applied MCQs**, not definition trivia. Test interpretation of a population difference, health vs healthcare equity, population vs individual prediction, aggregation/heterogeneity, external evidence vs local inference, and mechanism → measurement/intervention. At least one question must require transfer to a new laboratory/microbiology context.

## Progressive glossary

Show concise definitions only for terms actually introduced in 1.1 and link them later to the master glossary.

## References

Show **4–6 curated core references** to learners; retain the larger literature set in the evidence map/Evidence Library.

# Interaction QA gate

Every interaction must pass:

1. **Purpose** — Does the learner reason, predict, distinguish, investigate, or decide?
2. **Consequence** — Does feedback address a plausible misconception?
3. **Transfer** — Does it support a later laboratory decision?
4. **Evidence** — Is the claim supported or explicitly labeled **DIRECTOR LENS**?
5. **Accessibility** — Can the concept be learned without drag-and-drop, hover, animation, or color?
6. **Restraint** — Would static presentation teach it equally well? If yes, don't make it interactive.
7. **Pacing** — Does the interaction earn the learner's time?

## Build gate

Do not begin implementation until the storyboard is approved. Once approved, the next artifact is the **learner-facing content script**, followed by visual specifications, implementation, MCQs, evidence/reference QA, and usability polish.
