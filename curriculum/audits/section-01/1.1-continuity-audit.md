# Section 1.1 Continuity Audit
## From Health Disparities to Health Equity

> **Audit status:** First full-content audit
>
> **Scope:** Beats 1–11, before assessment writing and website implementation.

## Overall judgment

The lesson has a coherent reasoning arc and a distinctive laboratory-director identity. The FIT case successfully functions as a spine rather than a one-off vignette. The strongest through-line is:

**Notice → resist premature explanation → zoom out → use frameworks → PERSON → POPULATION → PLACE → MECHANISM → ACTION → return to the laboratory → solve the original problem again.**

The lesson should now be **compressed and polished rather than expanded**. Several concepts are intentionally repeated for reinforcement, but the current script risks exceeding the 10–15 minute target if every explanatory paragraph appears in the primary learner path.

---

# What is working

## 1. The opening problem earns the terminology
The lesson does not begin with definitions. The learner first encounters an ambiguous finding and discovers why distinctions among difference, disparity, inequity, and equity matter.

## 2. FIT is a strong longitudinal case
The case is understandable without specialty knowledge and naturally crosses:
- screening access,
- ordering,
- kit distribution,
- collection,
- specimen return,
- laboratory acceptance,
- reporting,
- follow-up.

It therefore supports both health-equity reasoning and laboratory systems thinking.

## 3. The laboratory remains relevant
The lesson periodically returns to the specimen and ultimately reframes laboratory data as the output of a selected diagnostic pathway. This prevents the module from becoming generic public-health instruction.

## 4. PERSON → POPULATION → PLACE → MECHANISM → ACTION works
The sequence has a clear cognitive purpose:
- **PERSON:** resist deterministic individual inference.
- **POPULATION:** inspect heterogeneity within categories.
- **PLACE:** test transportability/local relevance.
- **MECHANISM:** distinguish pattern from cause.
- **ACTION:** connect intervention to mechanism and reassess effects.

Keep this as the visual and conceptual centerpiece.

## 5. Curiosity is operationalized
“Get curious” is not merely a slogan. Learners repeatedly choose what they would ask, measure, or investigate next.

## 6. Nuance is generally preserved
The script avoids several common flattening errors:
- difference ≠ inequity,
- association ≠ causation,
- population ≠ person,
- group ≠ homogeneous,
- published elsewhere ≠ proven locally,
- local ≠ automatically unbiased,
- measurement ≠ intervention,
- contextual factor ≠ stereotype.

# Revisions before implementation

## A. Fix wording introduced when Population A/B became concrete
Beats 1–2 contain grammatical artifacts from the replacement, including constructions such as:

> “patients living in neighborhoods with higher area-level socioeconomic deprivation is experiencing…”

Revise these manually before publication.

## B. Shorten the visible learner path
The current content is richer than a 10–15 minute lesson if every explanation is always visible.

### Recommendation
Use three information depths:

**PRIMARY PATH** — question, decision, concise feedback.  
**WHY?** — optional expandable explanation.  
**GO DEEPER** — optional evidence/methods detail.

This preserves rigor without forcing every learner through every caveat.

## C. Reduce repeated “difference does not establish cause” language
This is the core principle, so repetition is appropriate, but several screens restate it almost identically.

Keep the strongest versions:
- **Observation ≠ explanation**
- **Variable ≠ mechanism**
- **Measurement ≠ intervention**

Let later screens build on these rather than re-explain them.

## D. Keep Beat 4 compact
The framework selector is useful but is the point most likely to feel like a public-health lecture.

For each framework, primary view should show only:
1. **Question it helps answer**
2. **What it organizes**
3. **One limitation**

Detailed framework descriptions belong in expandable content.

## E. Keep ACE to one job
ACE should remain a 60–90 second worked example of population association versus individual prediction.

Do not expand into:
- ACE screening policy,
- trauma-informed care,
- detailed ACE history,
- intervention recommendations.

Those would distract from the reasoning objective.

## F. Avoid an overly broad biomarker transfer example
The Beat 5 laboratory transfer is conceptually useful but may feel vague. During implementation, either:
- keep it as a very short transfer question, or
- replace it with a specific later-validated laboratory example.

Do not add a complicated reference-interval controversy to 1.1.

## G. Context-factor list should be discoverable, not a wall of text
Insurance, education, language, documentation-related barriers, incarceration, housing, transportation, work, caregiving, digital access, discrimination, healthcare availability, and policy are worth naming.

Present them as a compact expandable **“What else might matter?”** panel or visual constellation. The learner should not be expected to memorize the list.

## H. Clarify education versus health literacy
Do not collapse educational attainment/opportunity and health literacy into the same construct. They may relate, but they are not interchangeable.

## I. Use respectful, precise language around immigration/documentation and incarceration
Keep these framed as contexts through which policies, eligibility, continuity, fear, administrative burden, access, and systems may affect care—not inherent characteristics predicting patient behavior.

## J. Control / influence / partnership should not pretend to have universal answers
Beat 10 correctly recognizes institutional variation. In the final interaction, allow **CONTEXT DEPENDENT** and explain why a reasonable classification may differ by laboratory setting and role.

# Interaction audit

## Keep as true interactions
These require learner reasoning and earn interactivity:
- opening interpretation question,
- choose-three “get curious” questions,
- terminology classification,
- rewind diagnostic journey,
- framework selector,
- ACE prediction,
- open-the-category reveal,
- local/transportability challenge,
- mechanism-to-measurement matching,
- premature intervention choice followed by mechanism reveal,
- control/influence/partnership classification,
- final Know / Don't Know / Investigate Next challenge.

## Do not over-interact
These can be visual reveals rather than additional required clicks:
- every individual SDOH example,
- each framework limitation,
- every Reasoning Pitfall,
- glossary definitions,
- repeated five-part framework recaps.

# Terminology audit

Terms that require first-use definition or clear contextual explanation:
- FIT — already expanded.
- area-level socioeconomic deprivation — glossary exists; consider a one-line tooltip at first use.
- SDOH — spell out before acronym.
- ACE — now correctly expanded as **Adverse Childhood Experience** before acronym.

- ecological fallacy — keep concise; deeper methods in Section 2.

3. ACE population-association versus individual-prediction wording against Baldwin et al. and Austin et al.;

# Reasoning Pitfalls feature audit

The feature is valuable, but 25 separate pitfalls are too many to display as a flat end-of-lesson list.

### Recommendation
Keep the detailed list internally, but collapse the learner-facing end matter into approximately **8 high-yield pitfalls**:

1. **Difference ≠ inequity**
2. **Uncertainty ≠ irrelevance**
3. **Population ≠ person**
4. **Group ≠ homogeneous**
5. **Variable ≠ mechanism**
6. **Published elsewhere ≠ proven locally**
7. **Local data ≠ automatically unbiased**
8. **Measurement ≠ intervention**

Then allow **“See more pitfalls”** for the detailed set.

This gives the learner a memorable mental checklist without discarding nuance.

---

# Glossary audit

The progressive glossary is useful but should not interrupt the lesson.

### Recommendation
- Tooltip at first use for essential terms.
- End-of-lesson glossary for terms introduced in 1.1.
- Searchable master glossary later.
- Avoid adding a term solely because it appeared in author notes.

# Proposed visual identity for 1.1

Use one recurring visual language rather than unrelated illustrations:

1. **Dashboard** — observed pattern.
2. **Diagnostic pathway** — where processes occur.
3. **Zoom / nested context** — wider systems.
4. **Overlapping distributions** — person vs population.
5. **Opening a bar/category** — within-group heterogeneity.
6. **Two settings** — place/transportability.
7. **Branching pathways** — competing mechanisms.
8. **Intervention → consequences** — action/burden transfer.
9. **Same dashboard again** — synthesis.

The final dashboard should visually match the opening as closely as possible so the learner recognizes that **the data did not change; their reasoning did.**
