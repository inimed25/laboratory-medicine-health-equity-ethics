# 1.1 Evidence & Content Map — From Health Disparities to Health Equity

> **Status:** Evidence-map draft for review. This is not learner-facing lesson copy.
>
> **Purpose:** Define the conceptual foundation for the curriculum while teaching learners to interpret population differences without assuming causation, determinism, or universal generalizability.

## Proposed learning objectives

By the end of 1.1, the learner should be able to:

1. Distinguish health differences/inequalities, health disparities, health inequities, health equity, and healthcare equity while recognizing that terminology varies across sources.
2. Describe social and structural determinants of health and use established frameworks to organize potential mechanisms.
3. Explain why population-level associations should not be treated as deterministic predictions about individual patients.
4. Recognize heterogeneity within broad population categories and limitations of aggregation.
5. Explain why a disparity documented in one population, place, or health system should generate a locally testable question rather than a presumed local conclusion.
6. Separate observation from mechanism and mechanism from intervention.
7. Apply these principles to a diagnostic/laboratory pathway.

## Evidence labels

- **ESTABLISHED** — supported by established epidemiologic/public-health principles, authoritative frameworks, consensus/guidance, or substantial literature.
- **EVIDENCE-SUPPORTED APPLICATION** — evidence supports the principle, but application depends on setting/population.
- **EMERGING / DEBATE** — active methodological or conceptual discussion without one settled implementation.
- **DIRECTOR LENS** — educational synthesis created for this curriculum; not presented as a consensus framework.

## Proposed intellectual centerpiece: Person → Population → Place → Mechanism → Action

This is a **DIRECTOR LENS** created for the curriculum, not an established named framework.

### PERSON
**Population risk does not determine an individual's outcome.**

Use population evidence to inform clinical reasoning without converting probabilities into assumptions about a particular patient.

### POPULATION
**Group averages can conceal substantial within-group heterogeneity.**

Population categories can be analytically useful without being complete descriptions of individuals or communities.

### PLACE
**A pattern observed elsewhere may not transport unchanged to the local setting.**

External evidence should inform what to investigate locally rather than predetermine the answer.

### MECHANISM
**An observed difference does not establish its cause.**

Move from observation to plausible mechanisms and investigation before assigning explanation.

### ACTION
**Measuring a problem does not automatically identify the correct intervention.**

Consider mechanism, actionability, local context, stakeholder knowledge, tradeoffs, and unintended consequences.

### Director synthesis

> **Published evidence can tell us what questions may be worth asking. Local data and context help determine what is happening in our diagnostic system and what action is appropriate.**

## Proposed learner flow (target: ~10–15 minutes)

1. **Opening case:** Two populations show different rates of a diagnostic outcome. What can—and cannot—be concluded?
2. **Core terminology:** difference/inequality, disparity, inequity, equity, healthcare equity.
3. **Beyond healthcare:** SDOH and structural determinants.
4. **Framework selector:** Healthy People vs WHO vs ecological/nested models.
5. **Person → Population → Place → Mechanism → Action** interactive sequence.
6. **ACE worked example:** population association vs individual prediction.
7. **Local-context case:** same apparent disparity question in two different communities/health systems.
8. **Laboratory bridge:** trace how social/structural factors can enter a diagnostic pathway.
9. **Applied assessment:** 5–6 reasoning-focused MCQs.
10. **Takeaways + progressive glossary + curated references.**

## Proposed assessment targets

1. Distinguish an observed difference from a causal explanation.
2. Distinguish health equity from healthcare equity.
3. Interpret an ACE/population-risk example without deterministic inference.
4. Recognize within-group heterogeneity and limits of aggregation.
5. Decide what additional local information is needed before assuming a published disparity exists locally.
6. Identify where a social/structural mechanism could influence a diagnostic pathway.

## Writing guardrails for 1.1

- Do not imply that every observed demographic difference is an inequity.
- Do not treat association as causation.
- Do not use population statistics as deterministic descriptions of individual patients.
- Do not present broad demographic categories as biologically homogeneous groups.
- Do not assume a disparity demonstrated elsewhere necessarily exists locally.
- Do not dismiss external evidence merely because local confirmation is incomplete.
- Do not imply that identifying a social risk automatically establishes the appropriate intervention.
- Clearly distinguish established frameworks from this curriculum's **DIRECTOR LENS** synthesis.
- Use strengths/protective factors where appropriate without shifting responsibility for structural barriers onto individuals.
- Keep laboratory relevance visible so the lesson does not become a generic public-health textbook chapter.
