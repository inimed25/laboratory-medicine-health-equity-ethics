# Beat 7 — PLACE
## Published elsewhere does not automatically mean proven locally

## Transition

You now know two things:

**PERSON:** Population risk does not determine an individual's outcome.  
**POPULATION:** Group averages can conceal important variation.

Now imagine you find a well-designed published study reporting lower colorectal-cancer screening completion among patients living in socioeconomically deprived areas.

Your own FIT dashboard also shows a difference.

> **Have you now proven that the same disparity—and the same explanation—exists in your health system?**

---

## Screen 7A — Same question, different places

### Learner sees

A published finding sits at the top of the screen.

Below it are two fictional health systems.

### Health System North

- FIT kits are mailed directly to eligible patients.
- Return postage is prepaid.
- Instructions are available in several languages.
- Automated reminders are followed by navigator outreach.
- Most primary-care practices use the same screening workflow.
- Completed specimens can be returned by mail.

### Health System South

- FIT kits are usually obtained during an in-person visit.
- Return pathways differ by clinic.
- Some patients must return specimens to a clinical site.
- Reminder and navigation processes vary.
- Clinics differ in staffing and workflow.
- Screening documentation is fragmented across parts of the system.

### Prompt

**Would you expect a published disparity to appear identically in both systems?**

**A.** Yes. If the published study is rigorous, the local context should not matter.

**B.** No. The published evidence may identify an important question, but the magnitude and mechanisms of a local difference can depend on population, workflow, resources, policy, and setting.

### Best answer
**B**

### Reveal

> **Evidence travels with assumptions.**

A finding can be valid in the population where it was studied and still require careful evaluation before being transported to another setting.

---

## Screen 7B — What would you need locally?

### Prompt

Your institution resembles neither fictional system exactly.

Before concluding that the published explanation applies locally, **what would you want to know?**

### Learner action

Choose questions from several domains.

**POPULATION**
- Who is eligible for screening?
- Who is represented in the denominator?
- How are neighborhood deprivation categories constructed?
- Who may be missing from the dataset?

**PROCESS**
- How are FIT kits offered and distributed?
- How can patients return specimens?
- Where are failures occurring between order and laboratory receipt?

**PLACE**
- Do completion rates differ by clinic or neighborhood?
- Are transportation, mail access, clinic hours, or other local conditions relevant?

**MEASUREMENT**
- Is completion captured consistently?
- Are outside-system tests missing?
- Has the workflow or EHR changed during the measurement period?

### Reveal

No single question is sufficient.

**Local data are not merely a smaller version of published data.** They are produced by your own population, definitions, workflows, missingness, and measurement systems.

---

## Screen 7C — The two bad shortcuts

### On screen

Two doors:

### “IT WAS PUBLISHED.”
**Therefore it must be happening here.**

### “THAT STUDY WAS SOMEWHERE ELSE.”
**Therefore it tells us nothing about us.**

### Learner action
Reject both.

### Reveal

A more defensible path is:

> **External evidence → Local question → Local data + context → Comparison of plausible mechanisms → Decision**

### Director Lens

**Investigate, don't presume—and don't dismiss.**

Published evidence can tell you what may be worth looking for. Local investigation helps determine what is happening in your diagnostic system.

# Beat 8 — MECHANISM
## Same pattern, different causes

## Transition

Suppose your local analysis confirms that FIT completion really is lower among patients living in higher-deprivation neighborhoods.

You have now strengthened the **observation**.

You still have not established the **mechanism**.

---

## Screen 8A — “Why is this happening?”

### On screen

The same endpoint appears in the center:

**LOWER FIT COMPLETION**

Several pathways remain hidden behind it.

### Learner prompt

**Which explanation is most likely?**

Possible choices:

- patients are not receiving kits,
- kits are received but instructions are unclear,
- patients complete kits but have difficulty returning them,
- specimens are returned but rejected more often,
- outside-system completions are missing from the dataset,
- several mechanisms operate simultaneously.

### Learner action
Select an explanation.

### Reveal

Instead of marking one correct:

> **You cannot rank these mechanisms from the completion rate alone.**

Every option is a hypothesis that would require additional evidence.

### Purpose

Make the learner experience the difference between a plausible story and an evidenced mechanism.

---

## Screen 8B — What data would separate the stories?

### Learner sees

A diagnostic funnel:

**Eligible → screening addressed → FIT ordered → kit received → kit completed → kit returned → specimen accepted → result reported → follow-up**

### Learner action

Choose a suspected mechanism and then select the measurement that would best test it.

Examples:

**Hypothesis: kits are not reaching patients**  
→ Measure order-to-kit-distribution and delivery/receipt processes.

**Hypothesis: return is the major barrier**  
→ Measure kit-distribution-to-return attrition, stratified by relevant setting/context.

**Hypothesis: laboratory rejection contributes**  
→ Compare rejection rates and reasons among returned specimens.

**Hypothesis: the dashboard is incomplete**  
→ Examine outside-system screening, missingness, denominator construction, and data capture.

### Reveal

> **A useful next measurement is one that helps distinguish among competing explanations.**

More data are not automatically better data. Ask what uncertainty the measurement is intended to reduce.

---

## Screen 8C — The seductive variable

### Scenario

Your model shows that area-level deprivation remains statistically associated with FIT completion after adjustment for several measured variables.

### Prompt

**Does this establish deprivation as the mechanism?**

**A.** Yes. Persistence after adjustment establishes causality.

**B.** No. The association may remain important, but the measured variable can represent or correlate with multiple underlying pathways, and unmeasured factors or model assumptions may remain.

### Best answer
**B**

### Reveal

> **VARIABLE ≠ MECHANISM**

Statistical adjustment can refine an analysis. It does not transform a contextual variable into a complete causal explanation.

### Preview

Section 2 will return to measurement, confounding, categorization, causal reasoning, and what quantitative analyses can—and cannot—establish.

# Beat 9 — ACTION
## Now fix it

## Transition

Your team agrees the FIT completion difference deserves action.

Everyone is ready with a solution.

**Are you?**

---

## Screen 9A — Intervention roulette

### On screen

A set of plausible interventions appears:

- Rewrite patient instructions.
- Add automated reminders.
- Hire patient navigators.
- Mail FIT kits with prepaid return packaging.
- Retrain clinicians.
- Add an EHR alert.
- Extend specimen drop-off options.
- Change laboratory rejection criteria.
- Collect targeted process data before choosing an intervention.

### Prompt

**Which intervention should you implement?**

### Learner action
Choose one.

### First reveal

> **Not enough information yet.**

Several interventions are reasonable **for different mechanisms**.

A good intervention aimed at the wrong mechanism can consume resources without addressing the problem—and may create new burdens elsewhere.

### Headline

> **MEASUREMENT ≠ INTERVENTION**

---

## Screen 9B — Now you get mechanism data

### Fictional local investigation reveals

Among patients who receive a FIT kit:

- completion after successful return is similar across neighborhood-deprivation groups;
- laboratory rejection rates are low and similar;
- the largest difference occurs between **kit distribution and kit return**;
- qualitative feedback identifies difficulty returning kits during clinic hours and uncertainty about available return options;
- the difference is concentrated in clinics that require in-person specimen return.

> **Illustrative case data — not a published finding.**

### Prompt

**Which intervention is now most directly aligned with the evidence?**

**A.** Lower the laboratory positivity threshold.

**B.** Retrain laboratory technologists.

**C.** Expand convenient specimen-return pathways and clearly communicate those options.

**D.** Add more demographic variables to the dashboard without changing the workflow.

### Best answer
**C**

### Feedback

Now the proposed intervention is connected to an observed point of attrition and information about the local mechanism.

That does not guarantee success.

It gives you a **testable rationale**.

---

## Screen 9C — What could your solution break?

### Prompt

Before implementation, ask:

> **Who gains convenience—and who receives new work, cost, or burden?**

### Learner sees

Potential consequences:

- additional laboratory accessioning workload,
- added responsibilities for clinic staff,
- patient postage or transportation costs,
- digital reminders that exclude patients with limited portal access,
- navigation resources available only at selected clinics,
- longer turnaround from alternative return pathways,
- privacy or communication concerns.

### Concept

**BURDEN TRANSFER**

An intervention can improve one metric while shifting work, cost, delay, or complexity to another part of the system.

### Evidence label

**DIRECTOR LENS / EVIDENCE-SUPPORTED APPLICATION**

### Prompt

**What should be measured after implementation?**

Not only:

**Did overall FIT completion improve?**

Also:

**Who benefited? Who did not? Did the intervention create a new barrier or shift burden elsewhere?**

---

## Screen 9D — The action loop

### Visual

Build the curriculum-wide director action cycle:

**IDENTIFY → MEASURE → UNDERSTAND → DESIGN → IMPLEMENT → REASSESS**

Then surround it with:

**PARTNER**

because understanding and changing a diagnostic pathway may require patients, clinicians, laboratory staff, informatics, quality teams, community partners, administrators, or others depending on the problem.

### Director Lens

> **Action should be proportionate to what you know—and designed to teach you what you still don't know.**

# Reasoning Pitfalls — additions

### 14. Published elsewhere does not mean proven locally.
External evidence can motivate investigation without establishing the local magnitude or mechanism.

### 15. “Local” does not automatically mean unbiased.
Local data can contain selection, missingness, categorization, denominator, and measurement problems.

### 16. Do not confuse a plausible story with a demonstrated mechanism.
Several pathways can produce the same observed endpoint.

### 17. Statistical association does not make a variable the mechanism.
Measured variables may represent multiple underlying processes and remain subject to model assumptions and unmeasured factors.

### 18. Do not jump from measurement directly to intervention.
Interventions should be aligned with the mechanism the available evidence supports.

### 19. Improvement in the average can conceal unequal effects.
Reassess outcomes across relevant populations and settings after implementation.

### 20. Watch for burden transfer.
A solution can move work, cost, inconvenience, or risk from one part of the diagnostic system to another.

# Progressive glossary additions

**External validity** — The extent to which findings from a study are applicable beyond the population and setting in which the study was conducted.

**Generalizability** — The extent to which study findings apply to a broader target population under relevant assumptions.

**Transportability** — The extent to which an effect or finding can be carried from a study population or setting to a different target population or setting, accounting for relevant differences.

**Mechanism** — The process or pathway through which an exposure, condition, intervention, or system feature contributes to an outcome.

**Confounding** — Distortion of an observed association because another factor is related to both the exposure and outcome. This concept is introduced here and developed more fully in Section 2.

**Burden transfer** — **DIRECTOR LENS:** A curriculum term for situations in which an intervention reduces burden in one part of a diagnostic system while increasing work, cost, delay, inconvenience, or risk elsewhere.

**Reassessment** — Evaluation after implementation to determine whether an intervention produced its intended effects, for whom, and with what unintended consequences.

# Author / implementation notes

- All health-system descriptions and local FIT mechanism data are fictional educational scenarios.
- Do not imply that the fictional return barrier is the established explanation for socioeconomic differences in FIT completion generally.
- “Evidence travels with assumptions” is educational language, not a named scientific principle.
- Transportability terminology should be checked against the verified evidence map during reference QA.
- Burden transfer is explicitly labeled **DIRECTOR LENS** unless a more established term/framework is substituted later.
- Beat 8 should visually emphasize competing pathways rather than presenting one causal diagram as truth.
- Beat 9 should make the learner choose too early, then intentionally reveal why the choice was underdetermined.
- Avoid making “collect more data” the universally safest answer. Once mechanism evidence is adequate, the learner should be expected to act.
- The eventual UI should preserve the distinction between fictional case data, published evidence, and curriculum synthesis.
