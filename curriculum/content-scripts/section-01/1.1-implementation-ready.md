# 1.1 — From Health Disparities to Health Equity
## Implementation-ready learner script

> **Core-path target:** 12–15 minutes  
> **Case:** Fictional outpatient fecal immunochemical test (FIT) completion review  
> **Lesson principle:** Ask before explaining. If an idea returns, it must do new work.

### Content layers
- **PRIMARY PATH** — required learner experience.
- **WHY?** — optional explanation.
- **GO DEEPER** — optional evidence/methods context.

---

# Beat 1 — You found a difference

## PRIMARY PATH

**YOU FOUND A DIFFERENCE.**

During a laboratory quality review, your team examines completion of outpatient **fecal immunochemical testing (FIT)** after a kit is ordered for colorectal cancer screening.

| Patient population | FIT completed |
|---|---:|
| Patients living in neighborhoods with lower area-level socioeconomic deprivation | 82% |
| Patients living in neighborhoods with higher area-level socioeconomic deprivation | 64% |

**Illustrative fictional data**

The difference persists across several months.

### What does this finding establish?

**A.** Patients living in higher-deprivation neighborhoods are experiencing an inequity in diagnostic care.  
**B.** Biological differences probably explain the pattern.  
**C.** Clinicians are providing lower-quality care to patients living in higher-deprivation neighborhoods.  
**D.** A difference in test completion has been observed, but these data alone do not establish why it exists.

**Best supported: D**

> **OBSERVATION ≠ EXPLANATION**

The finding may matter. Start with what the data show, then investigate what produced it.

### Get curious

Before seeing more data, choose three questions you would ask next.

Examples:
- Who is represented in each category?
- What counts as completed?
- What happened between ordering and specimen return?
- Is the pattern present across sites?
- Are data missing differently?
- Could cost, transportation, language access, scheduling, accessibility, or workflow matter?
- What have patients, clinicians, laboratory staff, or community partners observed?

There is no required trio.

> Move from **“I see a difference, therefore I know what it means”** to **“I see a difference. What would help me understand it?”**

**Evidence label:** CASE-BASED REASONING / DIRECTOR LENS

# Beat 2 — Words change the claim

## PRIMARY PATH

The dashboard showed a **difference**.

Words such as *disparity*, *inequity*, and *equity* make additional claims.

### Match the claim

**DIFFERENCE / INEQUALITY**  
Measured outcomes are not the same.

**DISPARITY**  
A difference is meaningfully connected to disadvantage under the definition being used.

**INEQUITY**  
The difference reflects unfair or unjust conditions.

**EQUITY**  
People have a fair opportunity to receive and benefit from appropriate care.

> **Terminology varies across organizations and publications. Check how a source defines its terms.**

### One dataset, increasingly strong claims

**Difference**  
↓ adds relevant context  
**Disparity**  
↓ adds evidence and judgment about fairness/justice  
**Inequity**  
↓ points toward a desired state  
**Equity**

**Did 82% versus 64% give you enough information to climb the whole ladder?**

**No.** It established the first rung.

## WHY?

Calling something an inequity is stronger than noticing two different numbers. It requires additional evidence and context about opportunity, mechanisms, disadvantage, and fairness.

**Evidence label:** ESTABLISHED, with definitional variation

# Beat 3 — Zoom out

## PRIMARY PATH

A completed FIT specimen appears.

**This sample made it to the laboratory. What had to happen first?**

### REWIND THE DIAGNOSTIC JOURNEY

**Specimen received**  
← specimen collected  
← kit obtained  
← test offered/ordered  
← healthcare encounter  
← opportunity to access screening

> **Who appears in laboratory data can depend on what happened before testing ever began.**

Now zoom farther out.

Potential influences can operate across:

**LABORATORY / DIAGNOSTIC PROCESS**  
specimen requirements · accessioning · reporting

**CLINICAL ENCOUNTER**  
screening offered · communication · follow-up workflow

**HEALTHCARE ACCESS**  
appointments · coverage/cost · continuity · return pathways

**CONDITIONS OF DAILY LIFE**  
transportation · housing · work · caregiving · accessibility

**STRUCTURAL / POLICY CONTEXT**  
healthcare financing · institutional policy · neighborhood conditions · broader social/economic policy

A factor can span more than one level.

> **LEVELS INTERACT.**

### What else might matter? — optional expandable panel

Depending on the population, setting, and diagnostic pathway, relevant conditions may include:

insurance status and benefit design; income/economic constraints; **educational opportunity**; **health literacy**; primary/preferred language and language services; disability/accessibility; immigration- or documentation-related eligibility and access barriers; incarceration or recent incarceration and continuity of care; housing; transportation/geography; work schedules and paid leave; caregiving; digital access; experiences of discrimination or mistrust; healthcare availability/continuity; and institutional or public policy.

This is **not a checklist of explanations** and should not be attached automatically to an individual or demographic group.

> **The goal is not to memorize a list. It is to widen the questions you know to ask.**

## WHY?

Social determinants describe conditions and systems shaping health and healthcare—not inherent patient traits.

A variable such as ZIP code, insurance category, race, or an area-level deprivation measure may identify a pattern without explaining the pathway that produced it.

> **VARIABLE ≠ MECHANISM**

**Evidence label:** ESTABLISHED / EVIDENCE

# Beat 4 — Frameworks are tools

## PRIMARY PATH

You now have many plausible influences. How do you organize them?

**Choose the question first.**

| Question | Useful framework | What it organizes | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| What broad social domains should we consider? | Healthy People 2030 SDOH | Practical domains of social conditions | Does not establish the local cause |
| How can structural and intermediary conditions shape health? | WHO SDOH framework | Broader context, structural determinants, intermediary pathways | A plausible pathway is not proof it operates locally |
| At what levels might factors/interventions operate? | Ecological/nested thinking | Individual through policy/system levels | Levels overlap and interact |
| Where can influences enter diagnostic care? | Diagnostic Journey | Before, within, and after testing | Curriculum synthesis, not a consensus framework |

### Framework challenge

**Which framework tells you why the local FIT difference occurred?**

**None by itself.**

> **A framework helps organize questions. It does not substitute for answering them.**

**Diagnostic Journey evidence label:** DIRECTOR LENS

# Beat 5 — PERSON
## Population risk is not individual destiny

## PRIMARY PATH

### What is an ACE?

**ACE = Adverse Childhood Experience.**

Adverse childhood experiences are potentially traumatic or stressful childhood experiences captured in commonly used ACE measures.

At the **population level**, greater ACE exposure has been associated with higher average risk of multiple adverse health outcomes.

### Prediction challenge

A patient has a high ACE score.

What does the population association alone establish about this person's future health?

**A.** Poor health is probable enough to assume.  
**B.** The patient should be classified as unhealthy unless proven otherwise.  
**C.** The history may be relevant to risk, but the population association alone cannot reliably determine this individual's future outcome.  
**D.** Population evidence has no useful information.

**Best answer: C**

### Visual reveal

Show conceptual overlapping distributions for lower and higher ACE exposure.

> **POPULATION ≠ PERSON**

Group averages can differ while individual outcomes overlap substantially.

## WHY?

Baldwin et al. demonstrated the key distinction used here: ACE scores could distinguish average population risk while performing poorly at predicting which particular individuals would later experience poor health.

This does **not** make population evidence useless. Population evidence can identify patterns, generate hypotheses, inform prevention, and guide resource planning without becoming individual destiny.

**Evidence label:** EVIDENCE

## GO DEEPER

ACE is illustrative only. This lesson is not teaching ACE screening policy. The exact exposures included in ACE measures vary, and protective factors and unmeasured experiences also matter.

# Beat 6 — POPULATION
## Open the category

## PRIMARY PATH

Return to the FIT dashboard.

**Higher-deprivation neighborhoods: 64% completion**

What does 64% tell you about everyone inside that category?

It tells you the **aggregate pattern**. It does not tell you that everyone has the same circumstances or probability of completion.

### OPEN THE CATEGORY →

The single bar expands to show illustrative variation across neighborhoods, clinic access, distance, work schedules, preferred language, prior screening experience, and local workflows.

**Illustrative—not demonstrated mechanisms.**

> **GROUPS CONTAIN DISTRIBUTIONS, NOT CLONES.**

### Was the category useless?

**No.**

Aggregation can reveal important patterns. It can also conceal variation.

Disaggregation can sometimes clarify where a pattern is concentrated, but greater granularity can introduce small samples, unstable estimates, privacy concerns, and misleading comparisons.

> Ask **“What grouping is appropriate for this question and these data?”** rather than assuming either aggregation or maximal disaggregation is always best.

**Section 2 will develop this methodology further.**

# Beat 7 — PLACE
## Published elsewhere ≠ proven locally

## PRIMARY PATH

Suppose a rigorous published study reports lower colorectal-cancer screening completion among people living in socioeconomically deprived areas.

Your FIT dashboard also shows a difference.

**Has the published study established what is happening locally?**

**No.**

Compare two fictional systems:

**SYSTEM NORTH**  
mailed FIT kits · prepaid return · multilingual instructions · standardized workflow

**SYSTEM SOUTH**  
kits usually obtained in person · clinic-dependent return pathways · variable staffing · fragmented documentation

A finding may be valid where it was studied without appearing identically elsewhere.

### What would you need locally?

Consider:
- who is in the denominator,
- how FIT is offered and distributed,
- how specimens are returned,
- whether outside-system testing is captured,
- whether completion differs across clinics/places,
- missingness and measurement changes.

Reject both shortcuts:

**“It was published, therefore it must be happening here.”**  
**“It happened elsewhere, therefore it tells us nothing.”**

Instead:

> **External evidence → Local question → Local data + context**

## WHY?

External validity, generalizability, and transportability address related but distinct questions about whether findings apply beyond the original study population and setting.

**Evidence label:** EVIDENCE / DIRECTOR LENS

---

# Beat 8 — MECHANISM
## Same pattern, different causes

## PRIMARY PATH

Your local analysis confirms lower FIT completion in higher-deprivation neighborhoods.

You have strengthened the **observation**.

You still have not established the **mechanism**.

### Why is this happening?

Possible explanations include:
- kits are not reaching patients,
- instructions are unclear,
- return pathways create barriers,
- returned specimens are rejected,
- outside-system completions are missing,
- multiple pathways operate simultaneously.

Choose one.

### Reveal

**The completion rate alone cannot rank these explanations.**

Now match a hypothesis to information that could test it.

**Kits aren't reaching patients** → examine distribution/delivery.  
**Return is the major barrier** → examine distribution-to-return attrition.  
**Laboratory rejection contributes** → compare rejection rates/reasons.  
**Dashboard is incomplete** → examine outside testing, missingness, and denominator construction.

> **A useful measurement helps distinguish competing explanations.**

## WHY?

Even if area-level deprivation remains statistically associated with completion after adjustment, the variable itself is not automatically the mechanism.

> **VARIABLE ≠ MECHANISM**

Section 2 will develop confounding, measurement, categorization, and causal reasoning.

# Beat 9 — ACTION
## Now fix it

## PRIMARY PATH

Your team wants to intervene.

Possible ideas:
- rewrite instructions,
- reminders,
- navigation,
- mailed kits/prepaid return,
- clinician education,
- EHR alerts,
- expanded drop-off options,
- laboratory workflow changes.

### Which should you choose?

Choose before seeing more information.

### Reveal

> **NOT ENOUGH INFORMATION YET.**

Reasonable interventions address different mechanisms.

> **MEASUREMENT ≠ INTERVENTION**

### Now reveal fictional mechanism data

Among patients receiving a FIT kit:
- laboratory rejection is low and similar between groups,
- the largest difference occurs between **kit distribution and kit return**,
- patient feedback identifies difficulty returning kits during clinic hours and uncertainty about return options,
- the difference is concentrated in clinics requiring in-person return.

**Illustrative fictional data**

### Best-aligned next intervention

**Expand convenient specimen-return pathways and clearly communicate those options.**

This does not guarantee success. It creates a **testable rationale** aligned with the observed mechanism.

### Reassess

Don't ask only:

**Did overall completion improve?**

Also ask:

**Who benefited? Who did not? Did the intervention create a new barrier or shift burden elsewhere?**

## WHY?

A solution can move work, cost, delay, inconvenience, or risk from one part of the diagnostic system to another.

**Burden transfer — DIRECTOR LENS**

# Beat 10 — The specimen has a history

## PRIMARY PATH

A FIT specimen arrives.

Accessioning is correct. The specimen is acceptable. The assay performs as expected. QC passes. A valid result is released.

### Has the laboratory done its job?

The **analytical process may have performed correctly**, while important questions about the larger diagnostic pathway remain.

> **THE SPECIMEN HAS A HISTORY.**

### Rewind

**RESULT**  
← assay  
← acceptable specimen  
← return  
← collection  
← kit obtained  
← offered/ordered  
← healthcare encounter  
← opportunity to access care  
← social/structural context

### Play forward

**RESULT**  
→ interpretation  
→ communication  
→ follow-up  
→ treatment/prevention  
→ outcome  
→ future health-system/laboratory/public-health data

Processes before testing influence who appears in laboratory data.

Processes after testing influence whether a technically correct result produces clinical benefit.

### What is in your lane?

Classify examples as:

**CONTROL · INFLUENCE · PARTNERSHIP / ADVOCACY · CONTEXT DEPENDENT**

Cards can include assay validation, specimen criteria, reporting design, collection instructions, ordering pathways, navigation, transportation, insurance coverage, language access, correctional-health continuity, documentation-related access barriers, and community screening infrastructure.

There is intentionally no universal classification for every card.

> **Not everything affecting diagnostic equity is under laboratory control. That does not make everything outside the analyzer irrelevant to laboratory leadership.**

## WHY?

Authority varies by institution, laboratory type, jurisdiction, and role. The useful question is what a laboratory director can **control, influence, address with partners, or appropriately contribute to through advocacy/system redesign.**

### Optional micro-case: Don't turn context into labels

A chart notes a patient's language-access needs, Medicaid coverage, and recent incarceration.

What does that establish about why this individual did or did not complete testing?

**Very little without additional information.**

These facts can identify relevant systems questions or needed services without determining the individual's behavior, resources, preferences, understanding, or outcome.

> **CONTEXT CAN MATTER WITHOUT BECOMING DESTINY.**

# Beat 11 — Director Challenge
## Same dashboard. Different reasoning.

## PRIMARY PATH

Show the opening dashboard again with the same design and numbers.

| Patient population | FIT completed |
|---|---:|
| Patients living in neighborhoods with lower area-level socioeconomic deprivation | 82% |
| Patients living in neighborhoods with higher area-level socioeconomic deprivation | 64% |

**Illustrative fictional data**

The data have not changed.

### WHAT DO YOU KNOW?

- A measured difference exists in this dataset.
- It deserves investigation.

### WHAT DON'T YOU KNOW?

Build an uncertainty map:
- denominator validity,
- missing outside testing,
- within-group variation,
- differences across sites,
- point of attrition,
- mechanism,
- patient/staff perspectives,
- whether unfair/remediable barriers contribute,
- which intervention fits the mechanism.

### WHAT WOULD YOU INVESTIGATE NEXT?

If you can perform one initial analytic step, which most directly localizes where the difference emerges?

**Break the pathway into measurable transitions:**

**eligible → offered/ordered → kit distributed → returned → accepted/resulted**

and compare attrition.

> **Naming uncertainty turns uncertainty into an investigation plan.**

# Five takeaways

1. **Describe before you explain.**
2. **Translate population evidence carefully to individuals and subgroups.**
3. **Ask whether evidence applies in the local context.**
4. **Understand mechanism before selecting an intervention.**
5. **Treat the laboratory as part of a larger diagnostic system.**

# Reasoning Pitfalls
## Core eight

1. **Difference ≠ inequity**
2. **Uncertainty ≠ irrelevance**
3. **Population ≠ person**
4. **Group ≠ homogeneous**
5. **Variable ≠ mechanism**
6. **Published elsewhere ≠ proven locally**
7. **Local data ≠ automatically unbiased**
8. **Measurement ≠ intervention**

# Concise glossary

**Health difference / inequality** — A measured difference in health, healthcare, or related outcomes; terminology varies across sources.

**Health disparity** — A difference linked to social, economic, environmental, or other disadvantage under the definition being used.

**Health inequity** — A difference understood as unfair or unjust; definitions often also emphasize avoidability or remediability.

**Health equity** — A state in which people have a fair opportunity to attain health and receive appropriate care without avoidable, unfair barriers.

**Social determinants of health (SDOH)** — Conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live, and age, together with broader forces shaping those conditions.

**Structural determinants** — Broader socioeconomic, political, institutional, and societal arrangements shaping social position and downstream conditions.

**Area-level socioeconomic deprivation** — A contextual measure summarizing socioeconomic conditions within a geographic area; it is not automatically an individual's socioeconomic status.

**ACE (Adverse Childhood Experience)** — A potentially traumatic or stressful childhood experience included in frameworks studying relationships between childhood adversity and later outcomes. ACE scores are not deterministic predictions of an individual's future health.

**Within-group heterogeneity** — Variation among people or subgroups within a broader category.

**Aggregation / disaggregation** — Combining observations into broader groups / separating them into more specific groups for analysis.

**External validity** — Applicability of findings beyond the original study population and setting.

**Generalizability** — Applicability of study findings to a broader target population under relevant assumptions.

**Transportability** — Applicability of a finding or effect from one population/setting to a different target population/setting, accounting for relevant differences.

**Mechanism** — A process or pathway through which a condition, exposure, intervention, or system feature contributes to an outcome.

**Diagnostic Journey** — A DIRECTOR LENS used in this curriculum to trace influences before, within, and after laboratory testing.

# Implementation notes

- Preserve a visually identical opening and closing FIT dashboard.
- Keep all FIT percentages, local mechanism data, and fictional health-system descriptions visibly labeled **illustrative**.
- Use optional expansion rather than long default text for caveats and evidence detail.
- Essential terms should have tooltips at first use.
- Do not require learners to memorize framework diagrams or contextual-factor lists.
- Do not use red/green alone for answer feedback.
- Every required interaction must require prediction, distinction, investigation, or decision; avoid decorative clicking.
- The overlapping ACE distributions should be conceptual unless exact verified data are used.
- Avoid stereotyped imagery for adversity, poverty, immigration, incarceration, language, disability, or other social contexts.
- Education and health literacy are distinct constructs.
- Immigration/documentation and incarceration examples should remain focused on policies, access, eligibility, continuity, administrative burden, and systems—not presumed individual behavior.
- Final assessment MCQs are intentionally deferred until after this consolidated script is approved.
