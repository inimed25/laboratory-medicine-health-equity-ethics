# Beat 3 — Zoom out: Where could this difference come from?

## Transition

You know that FIT completion differs between patients living in neighborhoods with lower versus higher area-level socioeconomic deprivation.

You still do **not** know why.

So far, the dashboard has shown you the endpoint.

**What happened before that number appeared?**

---

## Screen 3A — Start with the specimen

### On screen

A completed FIT specimen appears in the center of the screen.

**This sample made it to the laboratory.**

### Prompt

Before the laboratory could report this result, what had to happen?

### Learner action

Select **REWIND THE DIAGNOSTIC JOURNEY**.

The interface moves backward one step at a time:

**Specimen received**

← Patient collected the specimen correctly

← Kit reached the patient / patient obtained the kit

← Testing was ordered or offered

← Patient reached an encounter where screening could be addressed

← Patient was eligible for and had an opportunity to participate in screening

### Reveal

The laboratory only sees the specimens that successfully reach it.

> **Who is represented in laboratory data can depend on what happened before testing ever began.**

### Purpose

Shift the learner from interpreting a laboratory denominator as if it were automatically representative of the underlying population.

---

## Screen 3B — Now zoom farther out

### Prompt

The difference could potentially arise at several levels. Where would you investigate each factor?

### Learner sees

Five nested layers:

**LABORATORY / DIAGNOSTIC PROCESS**  
Specimen labeling · specimen stability · accessioning · result reporting

**CLINICAL ENCOUNTER**  
Screening offered · communication · clinician recommendation · follow-up workflow

**HEALTHCARE ACCESS**  
Appointment availability · insurance/cost · continuity of care · ability to return a kit

**CONDITIONS OF DAILY LIFE**  
Work schedules · transportation · housing stability · caregiving responsibilities · health literacy

**STRUCTURAL / POLICY CONTEXT**  
Healthcare financing · institutional policies · neighborhood investment · broader social and economic conditions

### Learner action

Place a small set of factors into the layer where they most directly operate.

Do not make every factor have one rigid “correct” location. Some should reasonably span levels.

Example:

**Transportation**

A learner might place it under conditions of daily life.

### Feedback

> Reasonable. Transportation can directly affect whether a patient can obtain or return a test.

Then reveal:

> But its causes may also involve clinic location, transit infrastructure, institutional design, or broader policy. **Levels interact.**

### Purpose

Prevent the nested model from becoming a simplistic sorting exercise.

---

## Screen 3C — Social determinants are not patient traits

### Prompt

Which statement best describes a social determinant of health?

**A.** A characteristic that explains why an individual patient behaves a certain way.

**B.** The conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live, and age, together with broader forces shaping those conditions.

**C.** A list of demographic characteristics that can be entered into a risk model.

**D.** Any non-laboratory reason for a difference in test utilization.

### Best answer

**B**

### Feedback

Social determinants describe **conditions and systems**, not inherent characteristics of people.

A variable such as ZIP code, insurance category, race, or an area-level deprivation index may sometimes help identify a pattern. It is not itself a complete causal explanation.

### Key distinction

**VARIABLE ≠ MECHANISM**

A variable can point toward a question without telling you the pathway that produced the observation.

### Evidence

WHO; Healthy People 2030; Thayer et al.

---

## Screen 3D — Don't lose the laboratory

### On screen

Return to the FIT specimen.

Ask:

> If many influences occur outside the laboratory, is this still a laboratory-medicine problem?

### Learner response

**Sometimes yes—and not because the laboratory controls everything.**

The laboratory may be able to:

- measure where specimens are lost or rejected,
- identify differences in completion or turnaround,
- examine how collection and return processes work,
- improve instructions or specimen workflows,
- partner with clinicians, quality teams, patients, or communities,
- clarify what the laboratory data can and cannot establish.

### Director Lens

> **Responsibility does not require total control.**

Later in Section 1 we will distinguish what laboratory directors can **control**, **influence**, and address through **partnership or advocacy**.

# Beat 4 — Frameworks are tools

## Transition

You now have many plausible influences.

That creates a new problem:

**How do you organize them without turning “social determinants” into an endless list?**

Frameworks can help—but only if we know what question we want them to answer.

---

## Screen 4A — Choose the question first

### Instructions

You are investigating the FIT completion difference.

Select the question you want to answer.

---

### Question 1

**What broad domains of social conditions should our team consider?**

### Useful tool

**Healthy People 2030 — Social Determinants of Health**

Use it to organize domains such as healthcare access and quality, education access and quality, social/community context, economic stability, and neighborhood/built environment.

### Why it helps

It provides a practical vocabulary for looking beyond the clinical encounter.

### What it does NOT do

It does not, by itself, prove which domain caused your local FIT completion difference.

---

### Question 2

**How might broader social and structural conditions produce differences in health and healthcare?**

### Useful tool

**WHO conceptual framework for action on the social determinants of health**

### Why it helps

It distinguishes broader socioeconomic/political context and structural determinants from intermediary conditions through which health effects may occur.

### What it does NOT do

It does not tell you that a particular causal pathway is operating in your institution simply because the pathway is plausible.

---

### Question 3

**At what level might a factor or intervention operate?**

### Useful tool

**Ecological / nested-level thinking**

### Why it helps

It prompts consideration of influences at multiple levels—individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy/system.

### What it does NOT do

Levels are not independent boxes. A transportation barrier, for example, may reflect individual circumstances, clinic location, transit infrastructure, and policy simultaneously.

---

### Question 4

**Where could these forces enter the diagnostic process?**

### Useful tool

**Diagnostic Journey — DIRECTOR LENS**

**Social/structural conditions → exposure/disease risk → healthcare access → test offered/ordered → specimen collection → laboratory process → result → interpretation → ability to act → outcome → data generated**

### Why it helps

It translates broad equity concepts into questions laboratory professionals can investigate.

### Evidence label

**DIRECTOR LENS:** This is an educational synthesis used by this curriculum, not a published consensus framework.

---

## Screen 4B — Framework challenge

### Scenario

Your team notices that FIT completion is lower among patients living in higher-deprivation neighborhoods.

A colleague asks:

> “Which framework tells us why?”

### Answers

**A.** Healthy People 2030, because it lists the relevant determinants.

**B.** The WHO framework, because structural determinants are the most upstream explanation.

**C.** The Diagnostic Journey, because laboratory directors should use laboratory frameworks.

**D.** No framework establishes the local mechanism. Frameworks help organize questions and hypotheses that still require evidence.

### Best answer

**D**

### Feedback

Exactly.

> **A framework helps you ask better questions. It is not a substitute for answering them.**

Different frameworks can be useful simultaneously because they organize different aspects of the problem.

# Reasoning Pitfalls — 1.1 so far

These accumulate throughout the lesson and appear beside the glossary at the end.

### 1. A difference is not automatically an inequity.
Describe what was observed before assigning cause or fairness.

### 2. Uncertainty is not a reason to ignore a signal.
A difference can warrant investigation before its mechanism is established.

### 3. A variable is not a mechanism.
Race, ZIP code, insurance status, deprivation indices, and other measured variables may identify patterns without explaining the pathway that produced them.

### 4. Social determinants are not inherent patient traits.
They describe conditions and systems shaping opportunities, exposures, resources, and health.

### 5. Frameworks organize thinking; they do not establish causation.
A plausible pathway still requires evidence.

### 6. Levels interact.
Individual, clinical, organizational, community, and structural influences should not be treated as isolated boxes.

### 7. The laboratory sees a selected population.
Who reaches testing—and whose specimen successfully reaches the laboratory—can shape the data the laboratory ultimately observes.

# Progressive glossary additions

**Social determinants of health (SDOH)** — Conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live, and age, together with broader forces shaping daily life and health.

**Structural determinants** — Broader socioeconomic, political, institutional, and societal arrangements that shape social position and downstream conditions affecting health.

**Upstream / downstream** — Relative language describing where a factor sits within a causal or systems pathway; “upstream” generally refers to more distal structural/system conditions and “downstream” to more proximal processes or outcomes.

**Area-level socioeconomic deprivation** — A contextual measure summarizing socioeconomic conditions within a geographic area. It describes place-level context and should not automatically be interpreted as an individual patient's socioeconomic status.

**Ecological / nested-level framework** — An approach that considers influences operating across multiple interacting levels, such as individual, interpersonal, organizational, community, and policy/system contexts.

**Diagnostic journey** — In this curriculum, a Director Lens for tracing how influences can enter before, during, and after laboratory testing.

# Author / implementation notes

- The FIT case is fictional. Final UI should explicitly label illustrative numbers as fictional.
- Area-level deprivation is deliberately used to create an opportunity to teach that contextual measures should not be assigned to individuals as though they were individual-level attributes.
- Avoid presenting any framework as the universally “best” framework.
- Beat 3 should feel like a visual expansion of the learner's field of view, not a scrolling SDOH lecture.
- Beat 4 should be compact. Learners need to understand **what each framework is useful for**, not memorize diagrams.
- Full citations should be populated from the approved 1.1 evidence map during reference QA.
