# Beat 10 — The specimen has a history

## Screen 10A — What the laboratory receives

### On screen

A FIT specimen arrives in the laboratory.

Accessioning is correct. The specimen is acceptable. The assay performs as expected. Quality control passes. A valid result is released.

### Prompt

**Has the laboratory done its job?**

Possible responses:

**A.** Yes. Analytical quality is the laboratory's only responsibility.

**B.** No. A technically correct result is meaningless unless the laboratory controls every step of patient care.

**C.** The analytical process may have performed correctly, while important questions about the larger diagnostic pathway can still remain.

### Best answer
**C**

### Reveal

Analytical quality is essential.

But the specimen in front of you represents a patient who successfully reached a long chain of opportunities:

**screening eligibility → access to care → screening offered → kit obtained → instructions understood → specimen collected → specimen returned → laboratory acceptance → analysis → result communication → follow-up**

> **The specimen has a history.**

The laboratory does not control every step in that history. But understanding the history can change how laboratory data are interpreted and where laboratory professionals can contribute.

---

## Screen 10B — Rewind, then play forward

### Interaction

The learner drags or taps backward from **RESULT**:

**RESULT**
← assay
← acceptable specimen
← specimen returned
← specimen collected
← kit obtained
← test offered/ordered
← healthcare encounter
← opportunity to access care
← social and structural context

Then taps **PLAY FORWARD**:

**RESULT**
→ interpretation
→ communication
→ follow-up
→ treatment/prevention
→ outcome
→ future healthcare use
→ laboratory/health-system/public-health data

### Reveal

A laboratory result is neither the beginning nor necessarily the end of a diagnostic process.

Processes **before testing** influence who appears in laboratory data.

Processes **after testing** influence whether a technically correct result produces clinical benefit.

## Screen 10C — What is actually in your lane?

### Prompt

For each potential response, classify the laboratory director's role as:

**CONTROL** · **INFLUENCE** · **PARTNERSHIP / ADVOCACY** · **CONTEXT DEPENDENT**

Example cards:

- assay validation and analytical quality
- specimen acceptability criteria
- result reporting design
- collection instructions
- test-ordering pathways
- patient navigation
- transportation barriers
- insurance coverage
- language access
- correctional-health continuity
- immigration/documentation-related access barriers
- community screening infrastructure

### Reveal

There is intentionally no universal answer for every card.

Responsibilities and authority differ among institutions, laboratory types, jurisdictions, and roles.

### Director Lens

> **Not everything affecting diagnostic equity is under laboratory control. That does not make everything outside the analyzer irrelevant to laboratory leadership.**

The useful question is:

**What can I control? What can I influence? Where do I need partners? Where might laboratory expertise contribute to advocacy or system redesign?**

## Screen 10D — Don't turn determinants into labels

### Scenario

A patient's chart indicates limited English proficiency, Medicaid coverage, and a recent period of incarceration.

### Prompt

**What can you conclude from those facts alone about why this patient did or did not complete testing?**

### Best response

**Very little about the individual's mechanism without additional information.**

These characteristics may identify barriers that are important at a population or systems level and may appropriately prompt accessible services or further inquiry. They should not become assumptions about an individual's behavior, resources, preferences, understanding, or likely outcome.

### Reveal

Potential influences on healthcare disparities can include insurance and economic constraints, educational opportunity, language access, disability, immigration/documentation-related barriers, incarceration and continuity of care, housing, transportation, work conditions, caregiving, geography, digital access, discrimination, healthcare availability, and policy.

> **CONTEXT CAN MATTER WITHOUT BECOMING DESTINY.**

### Purpose

Make the SDOH discussion concrete while protecting against turning a list of determinants into stereotypes.

## Screen 11B — What do you KNOW?

### Learner selects all supported statements

- A difference in measured FIT completion exists in this dataset.
- Neighborhood deprivation caused the difference.
- Every patient in the higher-deprivation group encountered a barrier.
- The measurement deserves further investigation.
- The difference automatically constitutes an inequity.

### Best selections

**A difference in measured FIT completion exists in this dataset.**

**The measurement deserves further investigation.**

### Feedback

Start with the strongest claim the evidence supports.

---

## Screen 11C — What DON'T you know?

### Learner builds an uncertainty map

Possible unknowns include:

- whether the denominator represents all eligible patients,
- whether completion outside the health system is captured,
- how the deprivation measure was constructed,
- variation within each population,
- whether the pattern differs across clinics or neighborhoods,
- where attrition occurs in the diagnostic journey,
- what mechanisms contribute to the difference,
- patient and staff perspectives,
- whether the difference reflects unfair or remediable barriers,
- and which intervention, if any, would address the relevant mechanism.

### Reveal

**Uncertainty is not failure.**

Naming what you do not know turns uncertainty into an investigation plan.

---

## Screen 11D — What would you investigate NEXT?

### Prompt

You have resources for one initial analytic step.

Which option would most directly help localize where the completion difference emerges?

**A.** Compare the prevalence of unrelated chronic diseases between groups.

**B.** Break the screening pathway into measurable transitions—eligible → offered/ordered → kit distributed → returned → accepted/resulted—and compare attrition.

**C.** Immediately launch an educational campaign in higher-deprivation neighborhoods.

**D.** Remove neighborhood information from the dashboard to avoid overinterpretation.

### Best answer
**B**

### Feedback

This does not answer every equity question. It makes the diagnostic pathway more observable and helps identify where additional investigation should focus.

## Screen 11E — Assemble the Director Lens

The five concepts appear one at a time:

### PERSON
**Population risk does not determine an individual's outcome.**

### POPULATION
**Group averages can conceal meaningful heterogeneity.**

### PLACE
**Evidence may not transport unchanged across settings.**

### MECHANISM
**An observed difference does not establish its cause.**

### ACTION
**Measuring a problem does not automatically identify the correct intervention.**

Then place the diagnostic journey beneath them.

### Final prompt

> **You find a difference in laboratory or diagnostic data. What do you know, what don't you know, and what would you investigate next?**

No single click-to-reveal answer.

Give the learner a short text field or structured reflection space.

### Closing statement

> **Published evidence can tell us what questions may be worth asking. Local data and context help determine what is happening in our diagnostic system and what action is appropriate.**

# 1.1 End-of-lesson synthesis

## Five takeaways

1. **Describe before you explain.** An observed difference does not, by itself, establish disparity, inequity, or mechanism.
2. **Population evidence needs careful translation.** Group-level associations do not determine individual outcomes, and groups themselves contain substantial heterogeneity.
3. **Context matters.** Published findings can generate important local questions without automatically establishing the local answer.
4. **Mechanism matters for action.** Similar observed patterns can arise through different pathways and require different interventions.
5. **The laboratory is part of a diagnostic system.** Laboratory professionals can examine what happens before, within, and after testing while distinguishing control, influence, partnership, and advocacy.

# Reasoning Pitfalls — additions

### 21. Analytical quality is necessary but not synonymous with diagnostic equity.
A technically correct assay can exist within a diagnostic pathway that patients experience differently.

### 22. A list of social determinants should not become a list of stereotypes.
Insurance, education, language, documentation-related barriers, incarceration, housing, transportation, and other conditions may matter without determining an individual's behavior or outcome.

### 23. Do not confuse “outside laboratory control” with “irrelevant to the laboratory.”
Some problems require partnership rather than unilateral laboratory action.

### 24. Do not confuse responsibility with authority.
Laboratory directors should be clear about where they have direct authority, influence, partnership opportunities, or an advocacy role.

### 25. Naming uncertainty is productive.
Explicitly identifying what remains unknown helps determine what should be measured next.

# Progressive glossary additions

**Limited English proficiency (LEP)** — A term used in U.S. healthcare and policy contexts for people whose ability to communicate in English may be limited in particular settings. Language needs should be assessed directly rather than inferred from race, ethnicity, or appearance.

**Documentation-related barriers** — Barriers to healthcare access or participation that may arise from immigration/documentation status, eligibility rules, fear, administrative requirements, or related policy context. The specific mechanisms vary by jurisdiction and setting.

**Continuity of care** — The degree to which healthcare is coordinated and connected over time and across settings. Transitions into, within, and out of correctional settings can create continuity challenges.

**Diagnostic pathway / diagnostic journey** — The sequence of opportunities and processes through which a person moves from risk or symptoms to access, testing, interpretation, follow-up, and outcome. In this curriculum, the Diagnostic Journey is used as a **DIRECTOR LENS**.

**Analytical quality** — The performance of the measurement process itself, including characteristics such as accuracy, precision, reliability, and appropriate quality control. High analytical quality is essential but does not alone describe access to or benefit from testing.

# Implementation / author notes

- The context examples should remain non-exhaustive and should never be presented as a checklist that predicts an individual's behavior.
- Be careful with terminology around immigration and documentation status; laws, eligibility, and healthcare access vary by jurisdiction and change over time.
- Incarceration should be framed as a healthcare-system and continuity context, not an inherent patient risk trait.
- Language access should be distinguished from assumptions about literacy, intelligence, education, or patient engagement.
- The CONTROL / INFLUENCE / PARTNERSHIP-ADVOCACY activity intentionally permits context-dependent answers.
- Beat 11 should visually mirror Beat 1 so the learner experiences the difference in their own reasoning.
- The free-response closing prompt should not be graded for ideological conformity. If automated feedback is later added, it should look for evidence reasoning rather than preferred conclusions.
- Assessment MCQs should be written only after the full 1.1 continuity audit.
