# Laboratory Medicine, Health Equity, and Ethics

**Audience:** Pathology residents and fellows.

This module explores how laboratory testing can shape unequal opportunities for diagnosis and care. Cases connect access, assay design, interpretation, stewardship, quality improvement, and informatics with the pathologist's role in interdisciplinary discussion.

The aim is to develop thoughtful questions and recognize the limits of available evidence. Hypothetical scenarios are labeled; published findings and professional guidance appear alongside the relevant cases. Optional reflections and further reading offer additional depth.

# Section 1 — Foundations: From Health Disparities to Health Equity

**All FIT data in this section are hypothetical.**

## Beat 1 — What did we observe?

During a laboratory quality review, your team examines completion of outpatient fecal immunochemical testing (FIT), a stool-based colorectal cancer screening test, after a kit is ordered.

| Patient population | FIT completed |
|---|---:|
| Patients living in neighborhoods with lower area-level socioeconomic deprivation | 82% |
| Patients living in neighborhoods with higher area-level socioeconomic deprivation | 64% |

**Illustrative fictional data.** The difference persists across several months.

### Question 1

Which interpretation is best supported by the FIT completion data?

A. The groups differ in completion of a health care process; the data do not establish the cause or subsequent health outcomes.

B. Patients in higher-deprivation neighborhoods are more likely to develop colorectal cancer.

C. Patients in higher-deprivation neighborhoods have less opportunity to complete screening.

D. The clinics serving higher-deprivation neighborhoods provide lower-quality care.

**Best answer: A.** The dashboard measures testing completion. It does not directly measure opportunity, explain the difference, or establish differences in cancer outcomes. These possibilities may matter, but they require additional information.

## Beat 2 — Where does laboratory care fit?

Health and health care disparities are documented across populations and settings. Laboratory medicine is part of the care pathway in which these disparities can arise: who is offered testing, whether collection and return are feasible, and how results are interpreted and followed up.

The FIT dashboard gives us a starting point for understanding a difference in completion. Its importance does not depend on already knowing its full explanation. The central distinction is between identifying a pattern and understanding the processes that contribute to it.

### Match the concept

Match each statement to **health disparity**, **health care disparity**, **health inequity**, or **social determinants of health**. The examples are illustrative, not findings from the FIT dashboard.

| Statement | Your match |
|---|---|
| 1. Colorectal cancer mortality is higher in one population than another. | Select a concept |
| 2. Completion of follow-up colonoscopy after a positive FIT is lower in one population than another. | Select a concept |
| 3. An avoidable difference in health outcomes is understood to reflect unfair conditions. | Select a concept |
| 4. Housing, working conditions, and access to transportation shape opportunities for health and care. | Select a concept |

**Answer reveal**

1. **Health disparity:** A difference in health status or outcomes across populations.
2. **Health care disparity:** A difference in access to, use of, or quality of care across populations.
3. **Health inequity:** A difference in health understood as unfair or unjust; definitions often also emphasize avoidability or remediability.
4. **Social determinants of health:** Conditions in which people live, learn, work, and age, together with broader forces shaping those conditions.

These concepts overlap. Social conditions can contribute to differences in care and health outcomes, and some disparities reflect inequities. Definitions also vary across sources; the matches identify each statement's main emphasis.

**In the FIT case:** Completion is a health care process. The dashboard does not directly measure cancer incidence or mortality. The gap deserves attention while its contributing processes remain uncertain.

**Health care equity** describes a fair opportunity to access, receive, and benefit from appropriate, high-quality care without unfair barriers.

### Question 2

Which additional finding would most strengthen the interpretation that the completion gap reflects a health care inequity?

A. Both groups receive the same FIT kit and written instructions.

B. Patients at lower-completion clinics report that specimen-return options are difficult to use or collection instructions are unclear, and workflow data link those barriers to incomplete testing.

C. The gap remains similar when the dashboard is repeated the following year.

D. FIT results have comparable analytical accuracy in both groups.

**Best answer: B.** It connects the completion gap to barriers in how collection and return are organized or communicated. Persistence alone does not explain the gap, identical materials do not establish equal access, and analytical accuracy does not describe who can complete testing. This finding strengthens an interpretation of inequity without proving that these barriers explain the entire difference. The concern is the fit between the service and patients' needs, rather than an assumed limitation of a population.

Conceptual sources: [NIMHD — What Are Health Disparities?](https://www.nimhd.nih.gov/about/what-are-health-disparities) (2026), [WHO — Health equity](https://www.who.int/health-topics/health-equity), and [WHO — Social determinants of health](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/social-determinants-of-health) (2025). The matching activity uses working distinctions for this lesson, rather than claiming that every source separates these terms identically. NIMHD also includes differences in care among its disparity measures.

## Beat 3 — What happened before and after the specimen?

A valid result depends on more than an assay working correctly. For FIT, a patient needs an opportunity to access screening, receive an offer or order, obtain a kit, collect and return a specimen, and receive appropriate interpretation and follow-up.

Social determinants of health are the conditions in which people live, learn, work, and age, together with broader forces shaping those conditions. Structural determinants include institutional, economic, and policy arrangements shaping those opportunities.

In this fictional case, clinic hours, collection instructions, return options, or missing outside-system testing are possible explanations. None has been demonstrated by the opening dashboard.

<details>
<summary>What else can shape the diagnostic journey?</summary>

Relevant conditions may include insurance coverage and cost, preferred language and available language services, disability and accessibility, housing, working conditions and paid leave, caregiving, digital access, and availability of clinical services. Institutional policies and broader financing or eligibility rules can shape these conditions. Incarceration and immigration-related policies may also affect access and continuity of care.

These are possible influences, not assumptions about an individual or explanations established by a demographic category. Their relevance depends on the patient, setting, and diagnostic pathway.

</details>

**Optional reflection:** Where could a patient leave this pathway without ever appearing among the specimens received by the laboratory?

**Discussion:** Before an order, before obtaining a kit, or before specimen return. A dataset of received specimens cannot describe everyone who might have benefited from testing.

## Beat 4 — What does the category leave out?

The 64% completion rate describes a group, not every person in it. An area-level deprivation measure does not establish an individual patient's income, circumstances, understanding, or behavior. People within the category may have very different experiences.

### Question 3

A patient's address falls within the higher-deprivation category. What does the group completion rate establish about that patient?

A. The group pattern provides context, but does not establish this patient's circumstances.

B. The patient faces the same barriers as most people in the category.

C. The category has no useful role in understanding testing patterns.

D. The patient probably cannot understand the collection instructions.

**Best answer: A.** Grouping can reveal patterns while leaving important individual and within-group variation unknown.

## Beat 5 — What can the pathologist contribute?

The laboratory can perform FIT accurately while some people never obtain or return a kit, or never receive follow-up after a result. Laboratory teams contribute knowledge about how testing is designed, performed, and reported. Their authority and opportunities to contribute vary by setting.

| Relationship to the issue | Examples |
|---|---|
| Direct responsibility within the laboratory | Specimen acceptance criteria, assay performance, and reporting design |
| Influence through shared workflows | Collection instructions, specimen-return arrangements, and ordering pathways |
| Contribution through partnership | Discussions with clinical teams, patients, informatics colleagues, and community partners about access and follow-up |

These roles can overlap. Collection instructions, for example, may be managed by the laboratory in one setting and jointly with clinical services in another.

### Evidence across settings

Published findings can identify questions worth asking locally, but patient populations, resources, and workflows may differ between settings. External evidence can inform understanding without establishing that the same mechanism explains a local gap.

**Closing reflection:** In the FIT pathway, which part could your laboratory directly shape, and which would require collaboration?

**Discussion:** More than one response may be reasonable. The aim is to connect laboratory expertise with the relevant workflow and partners, rather than assign every access barrier to the laboratory.

The later QI section develops how changes can improve overall performance while disparities persist.

### Things to keep in mind

- **An observed disparity does not establish its cause.** Identifying a pattern matters even while its contributing processes remain uncertain.
- **A group average does not describe every individual.** Circumstances vary within demographic and geographic categories.
- **An accurate result is one part of diagnostic care.** Access before testing and interpretation and follow-up afterward also shape its value.

### Equity glossary

**Health disparity:** A population difference in health status or outcomes, interpreted using the source's definition; some definitions emphasize disadvantage and some include differences in care.

**Health care disparity:** A population difference in access to, use of, or quality of care, interpreted using the source's definition and relevant context.

**Health inequity:** A difference understood as unfair or unjust; definitions often also emphasize avoidability or remediability.

**Health equity:** Fair opportunity to attain health and receive appropriate care without unfair barriers.

**Social determinants of health:** Conditions and broader forces shaping how people live and their opportunities for health.

**Structural determinants:** Broader institutional, economic, political, and social arrangements shaping social position and opportunities.

# Section 2 — Who becomes visible in laboratory data?

**All counts and subgroup patterns in this case are hypothetical.**

A community sexual-health program offers home-collection kits for chlamydia and gonorrhea testing, using a collection system authorized for that setting. Its laboratory reports excellent specimen acceptance and turnaround time. The program also tracks earlier steps:

| Step | Patients |
|---|---:|
| Requested a kit | 200 |
| Kit dispatched | 180 |
| Specimen received by the laboratory | 110 |
| Valid result reported | 106 |

Dispatch does not establish that a kit was received.

### Question 1

What is the most important limitation of judging this program using laboratory acceptance and turnaround time alone?

A. The difference between kits dispatched and specimens received establishes that home collection is unsuitable for this population.

B. Those measures describe patients whose specimens arrived, while leaving earlier losses in the testing pathway largely unexamined.

C. High specimen acceptance suggests that patients who did not return a kit probably no longer needed testing.

D. Including all kit requests in the acceptance-rate denominator would reveal which barriers prevented completion.

**Best answer: B.** The laboratory can perform well for received specimens while many people never reach that stage. The counts locate incomplete transitions, but do not explain them. If reaching specimen receipt is more difficult for some patients, a dashboard beginning at receipt can conceal unequal access. These aggregate counts alone do not establish whether that occurred.

### Question 2

The program links kit requests with laboratory records and compares completion by housing circumstances:

| Step | Stable mailing address | Temporary or frequently changing address |
|---|---:|---:|
| Requested a kit | 100 | 100 |
| Kit dispatched | 90 | 90 |
| Specimen received | 70 | 40 |
| Valid result reported | 68 | 38 |

These fictional categories do not describe everyone's circumstances within each group.

Which interpretation is best supported?

A. Completion differs between the groups, with the largest measured gap emerging between dispatch and specimen receipt; delivery, collection, and return remain possible contributing steps.

B. Similar dispatch rates indicate comparable access; the difference mainly concerns patients' decisions after receiving a kit.

C. The lower receipt rate establishes that mailing failures are the main barrier for patients with changing addresses.

D. Similar specimen acceptance among returned kits indicates that the program is equitable once collection begins.

**Best answer: A.** The linked data reveal a gap that the laboratory's receipt-based dashboard concealed. Dispatch-to-receipt includes kit delivery, collection, packaging, and return. The data do not identify which contributed most.

### Shared debrief

Laboratory records describe an important part of the pathway, but not everyone who sought testing. Linking program and laboratory records makes earlier losses visible; stratifying those transitions can reveal differences in completion.

The pathologist contributes knowledge of specimen requirements, acceptance, and laboratory records. Program staff and patients contribute knowledge of delivery, collection, and return experiences. The case's address categories describe circumstances relevant to the workflow, rather than a patient's motivation or reliability.

**Takeaway: Who reaches the laboratory shapes what laboratory data can show.** Good performance among received specimens can coexist with unequal completion earlier in the pathway.

### Real-world basis

**Workflow feasibility:** FDA's 2023 authorization supports home-collected specimens shipped to a laboratory. It does not establish equitable completion.

**Documented completion differences:** Manavi and Hodson's UK observational study reported return of 3,099 of 5,310 requested kits (58.4%). Kits requested from less deprived neighborhoods were more likely to be returned in adjusted analysis. The design did not establish why patients failed to return kits.

**Patient experience:** A 2024 qualitative study of 100 service users found that convenience and privacy could facilitate postal testing. Some participants' living situations made home delivery less private, so collecting a kit elsewhere was preferable. Limited representation of digitally excluded people constrains its applicability.

**Benefits and scope:** A 2017 London randomized trial increased STI testing uptake with an online postal service (50.0% versus 26.6%). Eligibility required internet access and ability to read English. It does not establish access for excluded populations.

**Case boundary:** Our counts, address categories, and subgroup gap remain hypothetical. These studies do not directly demonstrate lower return rates among people with temporary or changing addresses. Neighborhood deprivation and housing instability are different measures.

References:

- [FDA — Simple 2 Test authorization](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/cdrh_docs/pdf20/DEN200070.pdf).
- [Manavi and Hodson — Factors associated with return of STI home sampling kits](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2017-017978). BMJ Open. 2017;7:e017978.
- [Spence et al. — How does online postal self-sampling shape access to STI testing?](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-024-19741-x). BMC Public Health. 2024;24:2339.
- [Wilson et al. — Internet-accessed STI testing: A randomised controlled trial](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1002479). PLOS Medicine. 2017;14:e1002479.

# Section 3 — Access to diagnostic characterization and its timing

A 2022 observational study found that, among patients with advanced nonsquamous non–small-cell lung cancer, **29.7% of Black patients versus 36.6% of White patients received NGS testing before first-line therapy**.

### Question 1

A colleague asks, “But both groups received a cancer diagnosis—what diagnostic disparity remains?” Which response best captures the concern?

A. The difference primarily concerns treatment delivery because the diagnostic process ended when lung cancer was identified.

B. The difference suggests that the original cancer diagnoses were less accurate among Black patients.

C. A diagnostic disparity would exist only if testing revealed actionable alterations at different rates between the groups.

D. The cancer was identified, but molecular characterization was available less often before treatment for Black patients, leaving potentially relevant features of their tumors less fully assessed.

**Best answer: D.** A diagnosis can correctly name the disease while leaving clinically relevant features uncharacterized. Here, the documented disparity concerns receipt of molecular testing before treatment—not demonstrated differences in diagnostic accuracy or the prevalence of actionable alterations. The testing measure does not establish when a completed report became available.

### Question 2

The study also reported lower NGS testing rates among Black patients **at any time during the study**, not only before first-line therapy: **43.8% versus 54.7%** among White patients.

Why does distinguishing “before first-line therapy” from “at any time” matter?

A. Testing completed later establishes that patients had equivalent access to molecular information when treatment began.

B. The two measures describe different opportunities: whether testing occurred during the observed period and whether molecular information could inform the initial treatment decision.

C. Testing before treatment primarily measures laboratory turnaround time, while testing at any time measures clinicians' ordering practices.

D. The difference between the measures establishes that delayed testing changed treatment outcomes.

**Best answer: B.** Eventually receiving testing is different from having molecular information available at an earlier decision point. An “ever tested” measure can miss unequal opportunities to use information when initial treatment decisions are made. Neither measure alone establishes why testing was absent or delayed, whether results were available when needed, or how treatment outcomes were affected.

### Shared takeaway

**A diagnosis can be established while access to clinically relevant characterization remains unequal.** Access also has a timing dimension: whether testing occurs and when its information becomes available describe different aspects of care.

### Real-world basis

The quoted rates come from a retrospective observational analysis of a real-world deidentified database. They document differences in testing receipt in the studied population and period. They do not establish that every untested patient had an actionable alteration, that testing would have changed every treatment decision, or that the observed gap has the same size in every setting today. The colleague's question is an educational framing, not a quoted study exchange.

Reference: [Disparities in Biomarker Testing and Clinical Trial Enrollment Among Patients With Lung, Breast, or Colorectal Cancers in the United States](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35737912/). JCO Precision Oncology. 2022;6:e2100427.

# Section 4 — Bias before the specimen: who is considered for testing?

Before a specimen reaches the laboratory, ordering rules and assumptions can shape who is considered for testing. Hemoglobinopathy testing is one example of this broader issue.

**Hypothetical case**

A pathology resident reviews a prenatal hemoglobinopathy-testing order set. Its decision support uses the patient's recorded race or ethnicity to determine whether to display a testing recommendation.

A colleague explains: “Some hemoglobinopathies are more common in particular ancestry groups, so this helps identify patients who need testing.”

### Question 1

What is the central equity concern with this approach?

A. Differences in condition frequency across populations have no relevance to laboratory medicine.

B. The main concern is whether patients can select more than one racial category; adding that option would resolve the limitation.

C. The decision support would be appropriate if the laboratory used the same analytical method for every specimen.

D. Recorded race or ethnicity may inadequately represent genetic ancestry, leaving some patients without an offer of testing despite potentially relevant carrier status.

**Best answer: D.** Population patterns can be informative, but a demographic category is an imperfect proxy for an individual's genetics. When that category determines who is offered testing, its limitations can affect access before a specimen is collected.

### Real-world basis

ACOG's 2022 advisory explicitly moved away from a race/ethnicity-based approach. It recommends offering hemoglobinopathy testing to people planning pregnancy or at the initial prenatal visit when prior results are unavailable for interpretation. The advisory identifies race and self-reported ethnicity as poor proxies for genetics. The order set above is hypothetical; the recommendation concerns this reproductive screening context and is not a claim that every laboratory test should be offered universally.

Reference: [ACOG — Hemoglobinopathies in Pregnancy](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/practice-advisory/articles/2022/08/hemoglobinopathies-in-pregnancy).

**Takeaway: A demographic shortcut can become a testing barrier.** The issue extends beyond how a laboratory analyzes specimens to how patients become eligible for testing.

# Section 5 — Reference populations, validation, and representation

### Case: Who informs the reference interval?

**Hypothetical case**

A patient's absolute neutrophil count (ANC) is **1,400/µL**, flagged below the laboratory's reference interval. Previous counts are similar. The patient has confirmed **Duffy-null status**, which is associated with lower circulating neutrophil counts.

During a laboratory consultation, a clinician asks whether the flag necessarily indicates a disease process.

### Question 1

Which response best explains the reference-interval issue?

A. A result below the laboratory interval establishes an abnormality requiring the same interpretation in every patient.

B. Duffy-null status means that a low ANC can always be dismissed.

C. A Duffy-null–informed interval may classify the count differently; the flag alone does not establish disease.

D. The interval should be selected using the patient's recorded race because it reliably identifies Duffy-null status.

**Best answer: C.** An accurately measured result can be flagged against an interval that does not adequately reflect a relevant biological characteristic. Confirmed Duffy-null status provides information that race alone cannot establish. It also does not exclude other causes of a low count.

**Real-world basis:** A 2023 study estimated a central 95% ANC reference interval of **1,210–5,390/µL** from 120 Duffy-null samples in a selected adult primary-care cohort. Participants self-identified as Black or African American; the study was not a representative sample of all Duffy-null populations. This illustrates why the population informing an interval matters; it is not a universal diagnostic or treatment threshold.

Reference: [Development of Duffy Null–Specific Absolute Neutrophil Count Reference Ranges](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10282887/). JAMA. 2023;329(23):2088–2089.

### Question 2

A research team uses a fixed minimum ANC to determine eligibility for a cancer trial. The threshold falls within the published reference interval for some healthy Duffy-null individuals.

What equity concern does this raise?

A. A single threshold ensures equitable eligibility because the same rule applies to every participant.

B. The concern can be resolved by applying a lower threshold to everyone recorded as Black.

C. A Duffy-null–specific reference interval establishes that every individual within it can safely receive the trial treatment.

D. Some individuals may be excluded because their baseline count falls below the threshold, even when that count is consistent with their Duffy-null status.

**Best answer: D.** A uniform threshold can affect people differently when their baseline distributions differ. A reference interval describes a population distribution; it does not establish treatment safety or replace a treatment-specific assessment.

**Real-world basis:** A 2024 cross-sectional analysis examined 289 phase 3 cancer trials and 71 curative-intent anticancer regimens. It found that 76.5% of the trials had explicit or implicit eligibility restrictions affecting ANC values within the Duffy-null–associated reference range. This was an analysis of criteria, not a measurement of how many Duffy-null patients were actually excluded or a treatment-safety trial. It supports the concern about how thresholds are used, rather than a universal recommendation to lower them.

Reference: [Cancer Trial Eligibility and Therapy Modifications for Individuals With Duffy Null–Associated Neutrophil Count](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2823538).

### Shared debrief

The first question concerns how a result is interpreted against a reference population. The second concerns how a threshold determines access. Laboratory expertise can help distinguish those uses and explain the relevant evidence to clinical and research teams.

### Things to keep in mind

- **The reference population helps shape what gets labeled abnormal.** A technically accurate measurement may be compared with an interval that does not adequately reflect a relevant biological characteristic.
- **Race does not establish Duffy status.** A demographic category cannot substitute for the patient's known biological characteristic.
- **A reference interval is not a treatment-safety threshold.** Being within an interval does not establish eligibility or safety for every intervention.

### Question 3 — What does the assay leave out?

**Hypothetical case**

A laboratory compares two targeted pharmacogenetic panels. Both accurately detect the variants they include. One tests only CYP2C9 *2 and *3; the other also includes *5, *6, *8, and *11.

A colleague asks: “If both have excellent analytical accuracy, why would the additional variants matter for equity?”

Which response best explains the concern?

A. Comparable analytical accuracy means the panels provide equivalent information across patient populations.

B. The broader panel matters only when a patient's recorded race establishes that they carry one of the additional variants.

C. Accurate detection of included targets can coexist with coverage gaps relevant to different ancestry populations.

D. More included variants necessarily make a panel clinically superior for every intended use.

**Best answer: C.** Accuracy for included targets and the scope of those targets are different properties. A negative finding on a limited panel does not exclude variants it never examined. Ancestry patterns help explain why coverage matters; they do not establish an individual's genotype.

**Real-world basis:** AMP/CAP’s 2019 CYP2C9 allele-selection recommendations include *2, *3, *5, *6, *8, and *11 in the Tier 1 minimum set, considering allele function, population frequency, and availability of reference materials. The additional alleles in this case are therefore not merely optional extras in that recommendation. Both panels are hypothetical; analytical accuracy for included targets does not establish adequate target selection. This example does not independently establish the representativeness of a clinical validation cohort or recommend treatment.

Reference: [AMP/CAP — Recommendations for Clinical CYP2C9 Genotyping Allele Selection](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7057225/).

**Takeaway: What an assay leaves out can matter as much as how accurately it measures what is included.**

# Section 6 — Test suitability in a particular setting

**All local workflow descriptions in this case are hypothetical.**

An adult community clinic serves many patients who previously received BCG vaccination. Work schedules and transportation make return visits difficult for some patients. The clinic and laboratory are considering options for testing for TB infection in patients for whom testing is indicated:

| Option | Proposed local workflow |
|---|---|
| Tuberculin skin test (TST) | Lower direct test cost, but requires a return visit for reading at 48–72 hours |
| QuantiFERON | Timely incubation requirements cannot currently be met through the proposed local workflow |
| T-SPOT.TB | Reference-laboratory testing is possible if transport meets the receiving laboratory's acceptance requirements |

These are fictional local constraints, not universal descriptions of vendor capability. The actual assay instructions and receiving laboratory requirements determine acceptable handling.

### Question 1

Which consideration most directly connects the choice of testing approach to equity?

A. Return-visit and specimen-processing requirements may create different barriers across the clinic's population.

B. A single test policy ensures that all patients experience the same access to testing.

C. The option with the lowest direct test cost minimizes the burden on patients.

D. A test collected during one visit eliminates the main sources of unequal access.

**Best answer: A.** A test's practical suitability includes what patients and the testing system must do to obtain a usable result. Equal policy, lower direct cost, or a single collection visit does not establish equivalent access.

### Question 2

The team favors a blood-based test because it avoids a return visit and is not affected by prior BCG vaccination. What limits that reasoning in the proposed workflow?

A. Avoiding a return visit makes completion more feasible, so local processing arrangements are a secondary consideration.

B. Those advantages depend on meeting the test's specimen-handling and processing requirements.

C. Lower direct cost favors the skin test unless the clinic documents how many patients miss return visits.

D. Reference-laboratory testing resolves local constraints once the clinic has arranged a courier service.

**Best answer: B.** Patient-facing advantages can be lost if the workflow cannot reliably produce a valid result. Tests for TB infection do not, by themselves, distinguish latent infection from active disease.

**Real-world basis:** CDC guidance describes the benefits of TB blood tests for BCG-vaccinated patients and patients unlikely to return for skin-test reading, alongside specimen-processing requirements and limitations. The case's specific clinic logistics are hypothetical.

**Takeaway: A test's practical suitability depends on the population and testing setting.**

### Things to keep in mind

- **Lower direct cost does not automatically mean lower patient burden.** Repeat travel, time away from work, and incomplete testing also matter.
- **Operational constraints are contextual.** Staffing, transport, and processing capacity can change.
- **Clinical usefulness extends beyond analytical performance.** A test must be accessible and capable of producing information that can be used in care.

References: [CDC — Clinical Testing Guidance for TB: Interferon Gamma Release Assay](https://www.cdc.gov/tb/hcp/testing-diagnosis/interferon-gamma-release-assay.html) and [CDC — Clinical Testing Guidance for TB: Tuberculin Skin Test](https://www.cdc.gov/tb/hcp/testing-diagnosis/tuberculin-skin-test.html). Product labeling and receiving-laboratory instructions determine the handling requirements for the specific collection and testing workflow.

# Section 7 — Diagnostic stewardship through an equity lens

More testing is not automatically more equitable care. Tests can provide useful information while also producing uncertainty, follow-up burdens, and costs. Fewer tests can prevent harm, but clinically indicated testing can also be missed. The equity question includes who benefits and who experiences the burdens of a testing pathway.

## Case: Who is selected for perinatal toxicology testing?

**Hypothetical case informed by published research.**

A pathology resident reviewing newborn toxicology orders notices that newborns of Black birthing parents are tested more frequently than newborns of White birthing parents. The laboratory uses the same assay and reporting process for everyone.

During an interdisciplinary discussion, someone asks: “If the laboratory handles every specimen consistently, where is the equity concern?”

### Question 1

Which response best explains the concern?

A. Different testing rates indicate that the assay's diagnostic performance differs between racial groups.

B. Comparing positive-result rates among tested newborns would establish whether the difference in testing was justified.

C. Consistent laboratory processing can coexist with unequal selection for testing, exposing some families more frequently to testing and its downstream consequences.

D. Testing rates are primarily an ordering issue; laboratory equity is better assessed through analytical performance and turnaround time.

**Best answer: C.** The laboratory may produce technically accurate results while participating in a testing pathway that affects populations differently. The disparity begins before the specimen arrives.

A confuses selection with assay performance. D overlooks the laboratory's role in understanding test use. B sounds reasonable, but results among selected patients cannot, by themselves, establish whether selection was appropriate.

### Question 2

An obstetric clinician asks whether testing every newborn would resolve the equity concern by removing discretion. Which response best captures the limitation of that approach?

A. Universal testing would address the equity concern because every newborn would have the same opportunity for detection.

B. Universal testing could reduce differences in selection, but its clinical value, consent process, and downstream consequences would still matter.

C. Universal testing would be justified if the laboratory confirmed every positive screening result before reporting it.

D. Universal testing would be justified if the overall positive-result rate remained high enough to support its use.

**Best answer: B.** Making testing universal could change who is selected, but equal testing is not automatically equitable or beneficial testing. Analytical confirmation addresses one source of uncertainty; it does not establish that testing everyone improves care. Likewise, a positive-result rate alone does not describe how results affect patients and families.

The pathologist's contribution is to connect the test's capabilities and limitations with its intended clinical use, in discussion with obstetrics, pediatrics, and other involved teams. This question does not establish that universal testing is the preferred policy.

### Real-world basis

A 2023 single-center retrospective study of 26,366 births from 2014–2020 documented racial differences in newborn drug-test ordering. Among births without prenatal urine drug testing, orders occurred in 7.3% of newborns of Black birthing parents compared with 1.9% of newborns of White birthing parents. The study did not capture testing indications and excluded births without prenatal care at that center. These findings support the selection concern without establishing why each order occurred or the same pattern in every setting. The case does not make a current jurisdiction-specific claim about reporting or consent requirements.

Reference: [Schoneich et al. — Incidence of Newborn Drug Testing and Variations by Birthing Parent Race and Ethnicity Before and After Recreational Cannabis Legalization](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2802124). JAMA Network Open. 2023;6(3):e232058.

### Why this belongs in stewardship

Stewardship includes the purpose and consequences of testing, alongside its frequency. In this case, the equity concern begins with who is selected for testing. Consistent specimen processing alone cannot resolve that concern.

### Things to keep in mind

- **Equal processing does not guarantee equitable testing.** Selection can differ before a specimen reaches the laboratory.
- **A difference in testing is a finding, not its complete explanation.** Ordering patterns need clinical and institutional context.
- **A positive result is not a complete clinical assessment.** Its meaning depends on the specimen, method, timing, medications, and clinical circumstances.
- **More detection is not automatically better care.** The consequences of identifying an exposure also matter.

## Brief reflection: When less testing also raises equity concerns

Reducing unnecessary testing can prevent harm and conserve resources. Yet a restriction may affect patients differently depending on how easily they can return, obtain specialist review, or complete an alternative test.

For example, a hypothetical policy requiring specialist approval for a send-out test may introduce little delay for an inpatient with an available consultation service, but a substantial barrier for someone whose next specialist appointment is months away. That difference does not establish that the restriction is inappropriate; it shows why overall reductions in test volume tell only part of the story.

**Optional, unscored reflection:** A laboratory reports fewer low-value send-out tests after introducing an approval requirement. What remains unknown about whether the change improved equity?

**Discussion:** The overall count does not reveal whether clinically indicated testing was delayed or forgone, who encountered those barriers, or whether accessible alternatives existed.

**Takeaway: Selection for testing distributes benefits and burdens.** Both unequal exposure to testing and unequal access to useful testing can matter.

# Section 8A — Quality improvement and laboratory action

**All counts in this case are hypothetical.**

## Case: HCV testing completion

A laboratory and its clinical partners introduce a workflow change intended to improve completion of HCV RNA testing after a reactive antibody result.

| Measure | Before | After |
|---|---:|---:|
| Overall completion | 130/200 (65%) | 180/200 (90%) |

### Question 1

Completion increased from 65% to 90%. A colleague describes this as an equity improvement. Which response best assesses that claim?

A. The increase supports an equity improvement because the same workflow was offered across settings.

B. Equity would be better assessed by comparing RNA positivity across settings than completion.

C. Completion improved overall; its distribution across settings remains unknown until the data are stratified.

D. The increase supports an equity improvement if the number of participating patients remained stable.

**Best answer: C.** The overall result establishes improved completion in the measured population. Comparing completion across settings can show whether that improvement was distributed similarly. Neither a shared workflow nor stable participation establishes that distribution, and positivity answers a different question. These before-and-after counts do not establish causation or treatment initiation.

### Completion by setting

The team then separates the results by clinic setting:

| Setting | Before | After |
|---|---:|---:|
| Hospital clinics | 90/100 (90%) | 98/100 (98%) |
| Community clinics | 40/100 (40%) | 82/100 (82%) |
| Overall | 130/200 (65%) | 180/200 (90%) |

### Question 2

What is supported by the stratified data?

A. Community-clinic completion did not improve because it remained below hospital-clinic completion.

B. Equal denominators establish equal opportunity to complete testing.

C. The workflow change corrected the cause of the initial gap.

D. Both settings improved and the absolute completion gap narrowed from 50 to 16 percentage points.

**Best answer: D.** The data show improvement in both settings and a narrower absolute gap. They do not identify the mechanisms or establish equal opportunity.

### Question 3

The team considers whether collection infrastructure contributes to remaining incomplete testing. Which finding most directly supports that possibility?

A. Missed collections clustered on days when required supplies were unavailable; observed collection practice was appropriate when supplies were available.

B. Staff in lower-completion clinics requested additional training.

C. Completion was lower in clinics that also had higher staff turnover and a different courier schedule.

D. A staff survey found variation in knowledge of the new workflow.

**Best answer: A.** This finding links missed collections to a specific operational constraint more directly than the other observations. It supports a hypothesis without establishing that supplies explain every missed collection.

### Optional outcome reflection

Testing completion, successful patient contact, referrals, treatment initiation, and time to treatment describe different stages. Measures restricted to patients who reach a later stage can leave out those lost earlier in the pathway.

### Participation and resources

Diverse participation can bring relevant perspectives into a QI discussion. Inclusion also involves whose perspectives influence decisions. One participant cannot represent every experience within a population. The same intervention may require different staff time, supplies, transport, or patient effort across settings.

### Real-world basis

CDC’s 2023 HCV operational guidance recommends single-visit sample collection and automatic HCV RNA testing after a reactive antibody result. It defines automatic testing as occurring without additional action by the patient or provider and cites implementation reports showing improved completion. The guidance supports the workflow premise; it does not validate this case’s invented clinic-setting gaps, supply findings, or treatment outcomes. The fictional before-and-after counts do not establish causation.

Reference: [CDC — Updated Operational Guidance for Implementing CDC's Recommendations on Testing for Hepatitis C Virus Infection](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/72/wr/mm7228a2.htm).

**Takeaway: Overall improvement can coexist with persistent disparities.**

# Section 8B — Informatics, AI data, and laboratory reporting

**Both cases in this section are hypothetical.**

## Case 1: What does the AI training label represent?

A team develops a model to identify a condition using EHR and laboratory data. Patients with a qualifying abnormal test result are labeled positive. All other patients, including those never tested, are labeled negative.

### Question

What is the central concern with this label definition?

A. Laboratory results cannot contribute useful information to an AI model.

B. A large dataset makes differences in testing access unlikely to affect the model.

C. Identical label rules ensure equivalent label accuracy across patient populations.

D. The model may learn patterns of being tested and having the condition detected, rather than underlying disease status.

**Best answer: D.** Untested patients are not established negatives. If testing differs across populations, the labels can encode those differences. This identifies a plausible mechanism; it does not establish harm from a deployed model.

A useful interdisciplinary discussion connects the data team's label definitions, clinicians' ordering context, and the laboratory's knowledge of canceled, rejected, or outside-system testing.

**Real-world basis:** Chang and colleagues (2024) studied Black and White adult emergency-department visits at two U.S. teaching hospitals. After matching on age, recorded sex, chief complaint, and triage score, Black patients had lower rates of CBC, metabolic-panel, and blood-culture testing; troponin testing showed the opposite direction. The authors discussed how testing-dependent labels could introduce AI bias. They did not evaluate harm from a deployed model or establish why each ordering difference occurred. The model and label definition in this case are hypothetical.

Reference: [Racial differences in laboratory testing as a potential mechanism for bias in AI: A matched cohort analysis in emergency department visits](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11524489/).

## Case 2: The result did not change, but the flag did

A patient's hemoglobin result is unchanged. After a legal-sex field is updated, the result changes from flagged high to unflagged. The patient is receiving testosterone. The laboratory and EHR maintain separate fields for gender identity, sex assigned at birth, and legal sex.

### What changed?

In this hypothetical case, the LIS selected a different reference interval using the updated legal-sex field. The unchanged value falls between the two upper limits. A flag reflects comparison with a configured interval; its change does not resolve clinical interpretation.

### Question

Which consideration best supports interpretation of the result?

A. Gender identity alone supplies the appropriate interval for every analyte.

B. Sex assigned at birth alone supplies the appropriate interval for every analyte.

C. Interval selection depends on the analyte and relevant physiology, including hormone exposure and duration.

D. Legal sex is the preferred physiological variable for all laboratory reporting.

**Best answer: C.** No single demographic field supplies a universal answer for all analytes. The case invites discussion of laboratory reporting and the evidence relevant to the specific measurement.

**Real-world basis:** A 2024 study examined how sex used for reference-interval assignment affects abnormal-result flags in patients taking gender-affirming hormones. ADLM’s 2026 guidance also explicitly discusses legal-sex fields, EHR/LIS logic, and hemoglobin reporting. Its recommendations for selected analytes address patients stably receiving hormone therapy for at least six months; interpretation varies by analyte and clinical context. A changed flag may be appropriate—for example, a male hemoglobin interval in someone stably receiving testosterone. The exact software event here is hypothetical, and the case does not establish patient harm or a measured population disparity. Its equity concern is whether reporting systems support appropriate interpretation for gender-diverse patients. ADLM guidance is not a CAP accreditation requirement.

References:

- [Impact of sex used for assignment of reference intervals in a population of patients taking gender-affirming hormones](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38737625/).
- [Providing Inclusive Care for Transgender Patients: Capturing Sex and Gender in Electronic Medical Record](https://academic.oup.com/jalm/article/6/1/210/6041066).
- [ADLM — Gender Diversity guidance](https://myadlm.org/science-and-research/academy-guidance/gender-diversity).

### Things to keep in mind

- **Improvement is not automatically improved equity.** Overall metrics can improve while existing disparities persist.
- **Not tested is not the same as negative.** Absence of a measurement does not establish absence of a condition.
- **A flag is a software comparison.** A measurement can be accurate while its flag is misleading because of an unsuitable reference interval.
- **A demographic difference is a finding, not its explanation.** Multiple clinical, social, and structural processes can contribute.
- **One perspective cannot represent an entire population.** Experiences vary within and between populations.

**Takeaway: Data and reporting choices shape what becomes visible.**

# Closing synthesis — The pathologist's role in equitable diagnosis

A test can be analytically accurate while its benefits and burdens are distributed unequally. Who receives testing, what an assay detects, which reference interval is applied, and when information becomes available all shape its usefulness in care.

Pathologists contribute by explaining both the information a test provides and its limits, in conversation with clinical teams, laboratory staff, patients, and informatics colleagues. That expertise can help others recognize when a testing or reporting choice may affect patients differently.

**Understanding a test includes understanding its limits—and whom those limits may affect.**
