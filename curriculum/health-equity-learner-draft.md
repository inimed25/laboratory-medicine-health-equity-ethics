# Health equity in laboratory medicine

A case-based space for learning, analysis, and discussion among pathology residents and fellows.

## Why this module?

Laboratory testing shapes opportunities for diagnosis and care. This module explores how access, testing choices, reference populations, reporting, and data can relate to health and health care disparities.

The purpose is to deepen interpretation and invite questions—not to present a single correct implementation plan for every setting.

## Learning objectives

By the end, learners should be better able to:

- Distinguish health disparities, health care disparities, health inequity, and social determinants of health.

- Recognize how laboratory workflows and testing decisions can create or reinforce unequal opportunities for care.

- Interpret case findings alongside published evidence, including what each does and does not establish.

- Discuss how pathologists can help clinicians understand test utility, limitations, and the implications of laboratory data.

# From health disparities to health equity

Explore what laboratory data reveal, what they leave out, and where pathologists can contribute.

## What did we observe?

During a laboratory quality review, your team examines completion of outpatient fecal immunochemical testing (FIT), a stool-based colorectal cancer screening test, after a kit is ordered.

| Patient population | FIT completed |
| --- | --- |
| Patients living in neighborhoods with lower area-level socioeconomic deprivation | 82% |
| Patients living in neighborhoods with higher area-level socioeconomic deprivation | 64% |

**Illustrative fictional data.**  The difference persists across several months.

### Question 1

Which interpretation is best supported by the FIT completion data?

**A**  The groups differ in completion of a health care process; the data do not establish the cause or subsequent health outcomes.

**B**  Patients in higher-deprivation neighborhoods are more likely to develop colorectal cancer.

**C**  Patients in higher-deprivation neighborhoods have less opportunity to complete screening.

**D**  The clinics serving higher-deprivation neighborhoods provide lower-quality care.

**Best answer: A.**  The dashboard measures completion of a health care process, not cancer outcomes or the reasons for the gap. This pattern is a useful starting point for investigation.

**Why the other answers are less appropriate**

- **B.**  The dashboard measures FIT completion, not colorectal cancer incidence. It cannot establish a difference in future cancer risk.

- **C.**  Unequal opportunity is a possible explanation, but the completion rates alone do not establish which barriers patients encountered.

- **D.**  A completion difference does not by itself establish clinic quality. Patient circumstances, workflow, and data completeness need investigation.

## Where does laboratory care fit?

Health and health care disparities are documented across populations and settings. Laboratory medicine is part of the care pathway in which these disparities can arise: who is offered testing, whether collection and return are feasible, and how results are interpreted and followed up.

The FIT dashboard gives us a starting point for understanding a difference in completion. Its importance does not depend on already knowing its full explanation. The central distinction is between identifying a pattern and understanding the processes that contribute to it.

### Match the concept

Match each statement to **health disparity** , **health care disparity** , **health inequity** , or **social determinants of health** . The examples are illustrative, not findings from the FIT dashboard.

Colorectal cancer mortality is higher in one population than another.

Completion of follow-up colonoscopy after a positive FIT is lower in one population than another.

An avoidable difference in health outcomes is understood to reflect unfair conditions.

Housing, working conditions, and access to transportation shape opportunities for health and care.

**Matching key:** 1. Health disparity; 2. Health care disparity; 3. Health inequity; 4. Social determinants of health.

These concepts overlap. Social conditions can contribute to differences in care and health outcomes, and some disparities reflect inequities. Definitions also vary across sources; the matches identify each statement's main emphasis.

**In the FIT case:**  Completion is a health care process. The dashboard does not directly measure cancer incidence or mortality. The gap deserves attention while its contributing processes remain uncertain.

**Health care equity**  describes a fair opportunity to access, receive, and benefit from appropriate, high-quality care without unfair barriers.

### Question 2

Which additional finding would most strengthen the interpretation that the completion gap reflects a health care inequity?

**A**  Both groups receive the same FIT kit and written instructions.

**B**  Patients at lower-completion clinics report that specimen-return options are difficult to use or collection instructions are unclear, and workflow data link those barriers to incomplete testing.

**C**  The gap remains similar when the dashboard is repeated the following year.

**D**  FIT results have comparable analytical accuracy in both groups.

**Best answer: B.**  The finding connects incomplete testing to barriers in collection and return. It strengthens the interpretation of inequity by examining how the service fits patients’ needs, rather than assuming a limitation of a population. It does not explain every incomplete test.

**Why the other answers are less appropriate**

- **A.**  Identical kits and instructions establish consistency of provision, but not whether collection and return arrangements are usable for different patients.

- **C.**  A persistent gap strengthens evidence that the pattern recurs; it does not identify unfair barriers or explain the pattern.

- **D.**  Comparable analytical accuracy concerns specimens that are tested. It does not explain unequal opportunities to obtain or complete testing.

## What happened before and after the specimen?

A valid result depends on more than an assay working correctly. For FIT, a patient needs an opportunity to access screening, receive an offer or order, obtain a kit, collect and return a specimen, and receive appropriate interpretation and follow-up.

Social determinants of health are the conditions in which people live, learn, work, and age, together with broader forces shaping those conditions. Structural determinants include institutional, economic, and policy arrangements shaping those opportunities.

In this fictional case, clinic hours, collection instructions, return options, or missing outside-system testing are possible explanations. None has been demonstrated by the opening dashboard.

**What else can shape the diagnostic journey?**

Relevant conditions may include insurance coverage and cost, preferred language and available language services, disability and accessibility, housing, working conditions and paid leave, caregiving, digital access, and availability of clinical services. Institutional policies and broader financing or eligibility rules can shape these conditions. Incarceration and immigration-related policies may also affect access and continuity of care.

These are possible influences, not assumptions about an individual or explanations established by a demographic category. Their relevance depends on the patient, setting, and diagnostic pathway.

**Optional reflection:**  Where could a patient leave this pathway without ever appearing among the specimens received by the laboratory?

**Discussion:**  Before an order, before obtaining a kit, or before specimen return. A dataset of received specimens cannot describe everyone who might have benefited from testing.

## What does the category leave out?

The 64% completion rate describes a group, not every person in it. An area-level deprivation measure does not establish an individual patient's income, circumstances, understanding, or behavior. People within the category may have very different experiences.

### Question 3

A patient's address falls within the higher-deprivation category. How should the team use this information when discussing FIT completion?

**A**  Use the group pattern to guide inquiry, and ask the patient which collection or return arrangements work for them.

**B**  Offer the support package associated with that category before asking about individual barriers.

**C**  Use the category to prioritize reminders, and infer that people who do not respond have similar return barriers.

**D**  Exclude neighborhood information from the service review because it cannot establish an individual explanation.

**Best answer: A.**  Area-level patterns can help identify where to investigate and offer support. They do not identify an individual patient's circumstances or preferences. **Why the other answers are less appropriate**

- **B.**  Offering support can be helpful, but assigning a package solely from a neighborhood category may miss the patient’s actual needs and preferences.

- **C.**  Population data can guide outreach, but failure to respond does not establish that individuals share the same barriers.

- **D.**  Area-level information remains useful for service review. Its limitations call for individual inquiry rather than discarding the population pattern.

## What can the pathologist contribute?

The laboratory can perform FIT accurately while some people never obtain or return a kit, or never receive follow-up after a result. Laboratory teams contribute knowledge about how testing is designed, performed, and reported. Their authority and opportunities to contribute vary by setting.

| Relationship to the issue | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Direct responsibility within the laboratory | Specimen acceptance criteria, assay performance, and reporting design |
| Influence through shared workflows | Collection instructions, specimen-return arrangements, and ordering pathways |
| Contribution through partnership | Discussions with clinical teams, patients, informatics colleagues, and community partners about access and follow-up |

These roles can overlap. Collection instructions, for example, may be managed by the laboratory in one setting and jointly with clinical services in another.

### Evidence across settings

Published findings can identify questions worth asking locally, but patient populations, resources, and workflows may differ between settings. External evidence can inform understanding without establishing that the same mechanism explains a local gap.

**Closing reflection:**  In the FIT pathway, which part could your laboratory directly shape, and which would require collaboration?

**Discussion:**  More than one response may be reasonable. The aim is to connect laboratory expertise with the relevant workflow and partners, rather than assign every access barrier to the laboratory.

The later QI section develops how changes can improve overall performance while disparities persist.

### Things to keep in mind

**An observed disparity does not establish its cause.**  Identifying a pattern matters even while its contributing processes remain uncertain.

**A group average does not describe every individual.**  Circumstances vary within demographic and geographic categories.

**An accurate result is one part of diagnostic care.**  Access before testing and interpretation and follow-up afterward also shape its value.

**Equity glossary**

**Health disparity:**  A population difference in health status or outcomes, interpreted using the source's definition; some definitions emphasize disadvantage and some include differences in care.

**Health care disparity:**  A population difference in access to, use of, or quality of care, interpreted using the source's definition and relevant context.

**Health inequity:**  A difference understood as unfair or unjust; definitions often also emphasize avoidability or remediability.

**Health equity:**  Fair opportunity to attain health and receive appropriate care without unfair barriers.

**Social determinants of health:**  Conditions and broader forces shaping how people live and their opportunities for health.

**Structural determinants:**  Broader institutional, economic, political, and social arrangements shaping social position and opportunities.

## Who becomes visible in laboratory data?

A community sexual-health program offers home-collection kits for chlamydia and gonorrhea testing, using a collection system authorized for that setting. Its laboratory reports excellent specimen acceptance and turnaround time. The program also tracks earlier steps:

### Question 1

What is the most important limitation of judging this program using laboratory acceptance and turnaround time alone?

**A**  The difference between kits dispatched and specimens received establishes that home collection is unsuitable for this population.

**B**  Those measures describe patients whose specimens arrived, while leaving earlier losses in the testing pathway largely unexamined.

**C**  High specimen acceptance suggests that patients who did not return a kit probably no longer needed testing.

**D**  Including all kit requests in the acceptance-rate denominator would reveal which barriers prevented completion.

**Best answer: B.**  Acceptance and turnaround describe specimens that arrived. Beginning the dashboard there leaves earlier losses unexamined. Track the full pathway to locate missed transitions, then investigate why they occur.

**Why the other answers are less appropriate**

- **A.**  A gap between dispatch and receipt identifies a transition to investigate. It does not establish that home collection itself is unsuitable.

- **C.**  Acceptance among returned specimens gives no evidence about whether people who did not return kits still needed testing.

- **D.**  Changing the denominator can describe overall completion more clearly, but it does not identify whether delivery, collection, or return barriers caused losses.

## A reported result is not the end of the pathway

**This local workflow and the patients are fictional.**

A pathology trainee joins a review of STI testing across a community clinic and a youth correctional facility. Both services use the same laboratory, which meets its reporting target. The community clinic’s usual follow-up pathway asks patients to check their portal or call to arrange care.

In this fictional review, some patients in custody cannot independently use that pathway, and some recently released patients do not have reliable private phone access or a stable address. Other patients can readily receive messages and arrange follow-up. These circumstances vary within each setting; incarceration or housing status alone does not establish an individual’s access.

### Question 3

What makes this an equity concern beyond the laboratory’s reporting performance?

**A**  A shared reporting target establishes comparable opportunity for follow-up, even if communication arrangements differ.

**B**  Patients released before results return can be counted as untreated because the facility cannot document subsequent care.

**C**  A pathway that relies on patients receiving messages and arranging care can create different opportunities to benefit from the same reported result.

**D**  Comparing turnaround time between settings would establish whether communication barriers caused a treatment disparity.

**Best answer: C.**  A patient-dependent follow-up pathway can create different opportunities to benefit from the same reported result. Examine communication access and coordination across release or transfer. Unknown follow-up must remain distinct from documented absence of treatment.

**Why the other answers are less appropriate**

- **A.**  Meeting a shared reporting target does not establish that patients have comparable access to messages or the ability to arrange follow-up.

- **B.**  Unavailable follow-up documentation means treatment status is unknown. It does not establish that a released patient went untreated.

- **D.**  Turnaround-time comparisons describe timing. They cannot alone establish communication barriers, their causal role, or a treatment disparity.

**Evidence and limitations**

[Wolf et al. — Screening for Chlamydia and Gonorrhea in Youth Correctional Facilities, Utah, USA (2024)](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/30/13/23-0712_article)

The study documents a transition before results returned and unknown treatment after release. It does not establish a follow-up disparity compared with community patients.

**Real-world basis and limits**

Wolf and colleagues studied screening in 12 Utah youth correctional facilities during October 2021–May 2023. Among youth testing positive for chlamydia, gonorrhea, or both, 25.35% were released before results were known. Those treated in custody received treatment within two days of results. Released youth were referred to local health departments; treatment data after release were unavailable.

This supports examining the transition between a reported result and care. It does not show that released youth went untreated, compare follow-up with community patients, or establish phone or housing barriers as the cause. The communication constraints in this teaching case are fictional.

## Access to diagnostic characterization and its timing

A 2022 observational study found that, among patients with advanced nonsquamous non–small-cell lung cancer, **29.7% of Black patients versus 36.6% of White patients received NGS testing before first-line therapy** .

### Question 1

A colleague asks, “But both groups received a cancer diagnosis—what diagnostic disparity remains?” Which response best captures the concern?

**A**  The difference primarily concerns treatment delivery because the diagnostic process ended when lung cancer was identified.

**B**  The difference suggests that the original cancer diagnoses were less accurate among Black patients.

**C**  A diagnostic disparity would exist only if testing revealed actionable alterations at different rates between the groups.

**D**  The cancer was identified, but NGS testing was received less often before treatment among Black patients, creating concern about unequal access to clinically relevant tumor characterization.

**Best answer: D.**  A diagnosis can correctly name the disease while leaving clinically relevant features uncharacterized. Here, the documented disparity concerns receipt of molecular testing before treatment—not demonstrated differences in diagnostic accuracy or the prevalence of actionable alterations. The testing measure does not establish when a completed report became available.

**Why the other answers are less appropriate**

- **A.**  Diagnostic characterization can continue after the disease is named. Molecular testing receipt is part of the diagnostic pathway even when it also informs treatment.

- **B.**  The study compares testing receipt, not the accuracy of the original cancer diagnoses. Lower receipt does not establish misdiagnosis.

- **C.**  Unequal access to clinically relevant testing can be examined without first demonstrating different actionable-alteration rates among those tested.

**Evidence and limitations**

- [Disparities in Biomarker Testing and Clinical Trial Enrollment Among Patients With Lung, Breast, or Colorectal Cancers in the United States](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35737912/)

Why does distinguishing “before first-line therapy” from “at any time” matter?

# Who is considered for testing?

## Bias before the specimen

Before a specimen reaches the laboratory, ordering rules and assumptions can shape who is considered for testing. Hemoglobinopathy testing is one example of this broader issue.

**Hypothetical case**

A pathology resident reviews a prenatal hemoglobinopathy-testing order set. Its decision support uses the patient's recorded race or ethnicity to determine whether to display a testing recommendation.

A colleague explains: “Some hemoglobinopathies are more common in particular ancestry groups, so this helps identify patients who need testing.”

### Question 1

What is the central equity concern with this approach?

**A**  Differences in condition frequency across populations have no relevance to laboratory medicine.

**B**  The main concern is whether patients can select more than one racial category; adding that option would resolve the limitation.

**C**  The decision support would be appropriate if the laboratory used the same analytical method for every specimen.

**D**  Recorded race or ethnicity may inadequately represent genetic ancestry, leaving some patients without an offer of testing despite potentially relevant carrier status.

**Best answer: D.**  Population patterns can be informative, but a demographic category is an imperfect proxy for an individual's genetics. When that category determines who is offered testing, its limitations can affect access before a specimen is collected.

**Why the other answers are less appropriate**

- **A.**  Population frequencies can inform laboratory practice. The problem is using recorded race or ethnicity as a gatekeeper for an individual testing offer.

- **B.**  Allowing multiple categories may improve demographic recording, but still does not establish an individual’s genetic ancestry or carrier status.

- **C.**  Consistent analysis applies after collection. It does not correct unequal access created by the rule deciding who is offered testing.

**Evidence and limitations**

- [ACOG — Hemoglobinopathies in Pregnancy](https://www.acog.org/clinical/clinical-guidance/practice-advisory/articles/2022/08/hemoglobinopathies-in-pregnancy)

See the case evidence note for the population, scope, and limitations of this source. Case interpretation does not establish an individual cause.

**Takeaway: A demographic shortcut can become a testing barrier.**  The issue extends beyond how a laboratory analyzes specimens to how patients become eligible for testing.

**Real-world basis and screening scope**

### Real-world basis

ACOG's 2022 advisory explicitly moved away from a race/ethnicity-based approach. It recommends offering hemoglobinopathy testing to people planning pregnancy or at the initial prenatal visit when prior results are unavailable for interpretation. The advisory identifies race and self-reported ethnicity as poor proxies for genetics. **Why the other answers are less appropriate**

- **A.**  Restricting to tested patients changes the development population. Performance in that subset cannot simply be assumed to apply to patients who were not tested.

- **B.**  Reweighting groups does not change the fact that untested patients have been assigned an unsupported negative label.

- **C.**  Subgroup evaluation against the same testing-dependent labels can reproduce the ascertainment problem rather than reveal accuracy for underlying disease.

**Evidence and limitations**

- [Racial differences in laboratory testing as a potential mechanism for bias in AI: A matched cohort analysis in emergency department visits](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11524489/)

The study supports reviewing testing-dependent labels; the proposed development steps are case-based reasoning.

A useful interdisciplinary discussion connects the data team's label definitions, clinicians' ordering context, and the laboratory's knowledge of canceled, rejected, or outside-system testing.

**Evidence and scope**

**Real-world basis:**  Chang and colleagues (2024) studied Black and White adult emergency-department visits at two U.S. teaching hospitals. After matching on age, recorded sex, chief complaint, and triage score, Black patients had lower rates of CBC, metabolic-panel, and blood-culture testing; troponin testing showed the opposite direction. The authors discussed how testing-dependent labels could introduce AI bias. They did not evaluate harm from a deployed model or establish why each ordering difference occurred. The model and label definition in this case are hypothetical.

Reference: [Racial differences in laboratory testing as a potential mechanism for bias in AI: A matched cohort analysis in emergency department visits](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11524489/).

## Case 2: The result did not change, but the flag did

A patient's hemoglobin result is unchanged. After a legal-sex field is updated, the result changes from flagged high to unflagged. The patient is receiving testosterone. The laboratory and EHR maintain separate fields for gender identity, sex assigned at birth, and legal sex.

### What changed?

In this hypothetical case, the LIS selected a different reference interval using the updated legal-sex field. The unchanged value falls between the two upper limits. A flag reflects comparison with a configured interval; its change does not resolve clinical interpretation.

### Question 2

What should the laboratory clarify before judging whether the changed hemoglobin flag is appropriate?

**A**  Whether the legal-sex update was entered correctly, then retain the interval selected by that field.

**B**  Whether the sex assigned at birth is recorded correctly, then compare the value with that interval.

**C**  The interval actually applied, relevant hormone exposure and duration, and evidence for interpreting this analyte in that physiological context.

**D**  Whether the patient has symptoms of erythrocytosis, then retain the default flag if none are reported.

**Best answer: C.**  First establish what the software did and whether the applied interval fits the analyte and relevant physiology. Hormone exposure and duration can matter for hemoglobin interpretation. Administrative-field accuracy or symptom review alone does not establish that the comparison interval is suitable. This is a reporting review, not a patient-specific diagnosis.

**Why the other answers are less appropriate**

- **A.**  Confirming the administrative update does not establish that its automatically selected interval is appropriate for this analyte and physiological context.

- **B.**  Sex assigned at birth alone does not resolve the relevance of current hormone exposure to hemoglobin interpretation.

- **D.**  Symptoms matter clinically, but their absence does not establish that the software applied an appropriate comparison interval.

**Evidence and limitations**

- [Impact of sex used for assignment of reference intervals in a population of patients taking gender-affirming hormones](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38737625/)

- [Providing Inclusive Care for Transgender Patients: Capturing Sex and Gender in Electronic Medical Record](https://academic.oup.com/jalm/article/6/1/210/6041066)

- [ADLM — Gender Diversity guidance](https://myadlm.org/science-and-research/academy-guidance/gender-diversity)

These sources support analyte-specific interpretation and review of EHR/LIS interval logic. The software event is fictional; the case does not establish patient harm.

**Evidence and scope**

**Real-world basis:**  A 2024 study examined how sex used for reference-interval assignment affects abnormal-result flags in patients taking gender-affirming hormones. ADLM’s 2026 guidance also explicitly discusses legal-sex fields, EHR/LIS logic, and hemoglobin reporting. Its recommendations for selected analytes address patients stably receiving hormone therapy for at least six months; interpretation varies by analyte and clinical context. A changed flag may be appropriate—for example, a male hemoglobin interval in someone stably receiving testosterone. The exact software event here is hypothetical, and the case does not establish patient harm or a measured population disparity. Its equity concern is whether reporting systems support appropriate interpretation for gender-diverse patients. ADLM guidance is not a CAP accreditation requirement.

### Things to keep in mind

**Improvement is not automatically improved equity.**  Overall metrics can improve while existing disparities persist.

**Not tested is not the same as negative.**  Absence of a measurement does not establish absence of a condition.

**A flag is a software comparison.**  A measurement can be accurate while its flag is misleading because of an unsuitable reference interval.

**A demographic difference is a finding, not its explanation.**  Multiple clinical, social, and structural processes can contribute.

**One perspective cannot represent an entire population.**  Experiences vary within and between populations.

**Takeaway: Data and reporting choices shape what becomes visible.**

## Case 3: Covered testing, unequal privacy

**This patient and local workflow are fictional.**

During an interdisciplinary review, a pathology trainee hears about a 22-year-old patient considering indicated chlamydia and gonorrhea testing while covered by a parent’s insurance plan. The patient is concerned that a billing communication might reveal the visit or testing to the policyholder. The clinic can collect the specimen and the laboratory can perform the assay, but the patient asks to postpone testing until the communication pathway is clarified.

The laboratory’s dashboard begins with specimens received and shows no processing problem. The review includes laboratory, clinical, billing, and privacy staff because each sees a different part of the pathway.

### Question 3

What does this case add to the interpretation of testing access?

**A**  The concern is primarily administrative, so it falls outside an assessment of equitable testing access.

**B**  Coverage and assay availability do not establish usable access when anticipated disclosure may carry different consequences for different patients.

**C**  The patient’s decision establishes that billing disclosures are the cause of lower STI testing rates among insured young adults.

**D**  A confidential laboratory result would resolve the concern because billing communications are separate from the diagnostic pathway.

**Best answer: B.**  Usable access includes the ability to seek testing with acceptable privacy. Laboratory-result confidentiality and billing communications involve different systems. This patient’s concern warrants clarification with the relevant teams; it does not establish a population-level cause.

**Why the other answers are less appropriate**

- **A.**  Administrative communications can influence whether patients seek testing. They are relevant to usable diagnostic access even when the assay is available.

- **C.**  One fictional patient’s decision illustrates a possible barrier. It cannot establish the cause of population-level testing differences.

- **D.**  Protecting the laboratory result does not necessarily prevent disclosure through separate billing communications. Both pathways require clarification.

**Evidence and limitations**

- [Leichliter et al. — Confidentiality Issues and Use of STD Services (CDC, 2017)](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/66/wr/mm6609a1.htm)

- [HHS — Uses and Disclosures for Treatment, Payment, and Health Care Operations](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/disclosures-treatment-payment-health-care-operations/index.html)

The CDC study supports the confidentiality-and-access concern; HHS guidance provides communication and payment context. Neither documents this fictional incident.

**Evidence and scope**

A 2017 CDC analysis of the 2013–2015 National Survey of Family Growth found that confidentiality concerns were associated with lower self-reported chlamydia testing among female respondents. Its confidentiality question covered sexually experienced respondents aged 15–17 and those aged 18–25 on a parent’s private insurance plan. The cross-sectional survey did not establish causation or isolate billing communications as the mechanism.

HHS guidance describes permitted treatment and payment disclosures and confidential-communication requests. Options depend on the applicable rules and setting; this case does not establish a legal violation or recommend a blanket delay of result release.

**Reflection:**  What becomes invisible when a laboratory measures only patients whose specimens arrive? How does concern about disclosure differ from inability to pay?

**Coverage is not automatically usable access.**  Financial protection and privacy can affect the same testing decision in different ways.

## The pathologist's role in equitable diagnosis

A test can be analytically accurate while its benefits and burdens are distributed unequally. Who receives testing, what an assay detects, which reference interval is applied, and when information becomes available all shape its usefulness in care.

Pathologists contribute by explaining both the information a test provides and its limits, in conversation with clinical teams, laboratory staff, patients, and informatics colleagues. That expertise can help others recognize when a testing or reporting choice may affect patients differently.

**Understanding a test includes understanding its limits—and whom those limits may affect.**

## Apply this to a testing pathway you know

Optional, unscored exercise: choose one familiar test and sketch the pathway from considering testing to acting on the result. You can think through the prompts or make your own notes; this page does not collect them.

- **Who is missing?**  Who might never appear in the laboratory dashboard, and what is its starting denominator?

- **Where might burdens differ?**  Consider collection, cost, language, transport, privacy, and the ability to receive or act on results.

- **What would you investigate?**  Name one additional data source and one perspective that would help distinguish possible explanations.

- **Who should help?**  Identify relevant laboratory staff, clinical or operational partners, and patients or community members. Whose input would influence the decision?

- **What would you monitor?**  Choose a process measure, a stratified outcome, and a balancing measure for one small proposed change.

**One possible approach: a specimen-return pathway**

Begin with all kit requests, then distinguish dispatch, confirmed delivery where available, specimen receipt, valid result, and follow-up. Combine records with feedback from people whose kits were not returned. A small change might be offering a locally feasible return option selected with patients and staff. Track use of that option, completion across relevant groups, and added travel or staff workload. This is an investigation plan, not evidence that the change will work everywhere.

You do not need to prove the full cause of a disparity before asking a useful question. State what you know, what remains uncertain, and how the next step could improve understanding.
